Compare diets studied in research (optional)

These are summaries of studies, not a diet chosen for you. Study results describe groups, not how your body will respond. Discuss the options with your clinician or registered dietitian, especially if you take diabetes medicines or have kidney disease, are pregnant, or have an eating disorder history.

Mediterranean-style

Vegetables, beans, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and other unsaturated fats, with flexible regional food choices. Trials have found cardiovascular benefits in studied populations.

Evidence: PREDIMED (2018 reanalysis) and CORDIOPREV (2022). The original 2013 PREDIMED report was retracted because of randomization irregularities; use the corrected 2018 report.

PREDIMED reanalysis · CORDIOPREV trial

DASH

A food pattern rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains and lower-fat dairy or suitable alternatives. Studied mainly for blood pressure, including effects of sodium reduction.

Limit: the site's lab form does not measure blood pressure; a cholesterol or glucose flag is not a reason to start a personalized low-sodium plan.

DASH-Sodium randomized trial

Portfolio diet

Combines foods such as oats and other viscous-fiber sources, nuts, plant proteins and plant sterols. Trials in people with elevated cholesterol found reductions in LDL-C.

Limit: LDL changes do not prove this particular diet prevents cardiovascular events or replaces medical care for very high LDL.

2018 controlled-trials meta-analysis

Moderate lower-carbohydrate

Emphasizes carbohydrate quality and portion choices; it is not the same as a ketogenic diet. Some studies report short-term improvements in glycemia and triglycerides; long-term superiority is uncertain.

Limit: restrictions can change medication needs; do not stop or adjust medicines yourself. Food quality and sustainability matter.

ADA 2026 nutrition guidance · 2021 systematic review

Ketogenic (very low carbohydrate)

In a small 2022 randomized crossover trial comparing keto with low-carb Mediterranean diets, the primary HbA1c difference was not significant. Triglycerides favored keto; LDL-C, fiber and some nutrient intakes favored Mediterranean.

Important safety: a ketogenic diet is not automatically recommended from lab flags. It may be unsafe with SGLT2 diabetes medicines, some other glucose-lowering treatments, kidney disease, pregnancy or disordered-eating risk. Discuss before attempting a restrictive plan.

Keto-Med 2022 trial · ADA 2026 safety guidance

Plant-forward / healthy low-fat

A flexible pattern emphasizing minimally processed plant foods and suitable proteins. In the DIETFITS trial, healthy low-fat and healthy low-carb groups had no significant difference in 12-month weight loss.

Limit: that trial did not validate choosing a diet from ancestry, a small genotype panel or an insulin measurement.

DIETFITS randomized trial

Time-restricted eating (timing strategy)

Changes when food is eaten, not necessarily which foods. In one 12-month trial, adding an 8-hour eating window to calorie restriction did not produce significantly greater weight loss than calorie restriction alone.

Important safety: fasting can interact with diabetes medications, hypoglycemia and other conditions; plan it with a clinician if these apply.

2022 randomized trial · ADA 2026 nutrition guidance

Measurement tips: use a soft tape; measure the wrist at its narrowest point just beyond the wrist bone (toward the hand) (MedlinePlus). Elbow breadth is the width between the two bony points of the elbow with the arm bent at 90°, ideally with a caliper; a tape against a ruler is less accurate. Errors of a tenth of an inch can change the frame result near a cut-off.

About these numbers. This is an educational estimate, not medical advice. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, and the cut-offs are less reliable for very muscular people, people 65 and over, and during pregnancy (do not use it if pregnant). Ancestry cut-offs and gene notes describe population averages, not you personally. Frame size from a tape measure is approximate, and the ideal-weight formulas date from mid-20th-century US data. For these reasons the page gives ranges, not a single "perfect" weight. If you have a medical condition, check any weight or diet change with your doctor or a registered dietitian.
Limitations and how this calculator works
  • Healthy range: BMI 18.5 up to the population's overweight cut-off (25 under WHO international cut-offs; 23 for groups NICE and WHO 2004 assign lower thresholds). The upper edge shown is 0.1 below the cut-off.
  • Frame size: wrist uses the height/wrist ratio cut-offs (Grant 1980, cited via secondary sources); elbow uses the 1983 Metropolitan Life table (heights in 1-inch heels). When the two disagree, both frames are kept and the range widens.
  • Suggested range: the overlap between the four classic ideal-body-weight formulas (Hamwi with ±10% for frame, Devine, Robinson, Miller) and the healthy range. This combination is this calculator's own method, not a clinical standard.
  • Mixed ancestry: by default the cut-offs follow the background you identify with: the ADA 2026 obesity standards note an evidence gap for mixed race or ethnicity and tell clinicians to use the background with which the person identifies. Until you pick one, the background with the largest share is used (if two are equal, the first one entered). Every cut-off on the page (ranges, diabetes buffer zone, waist thresholds) then follows that background. Alternatively you can blend the overweight and obesity cut-offs in proportion to the percentages you enter (e.g., 50% in a lower-cut-off group gives 24 / 28.8); no guideline defines this, so the blend is the calculator's own method, and the standard and lower ranges are shown alongside it.
  • Waist (optional): shown as waist-to-height ratio (NICE) and as the ADA 2026 overweight/obesity diagnosis: with an Asian background, BMI 23-27.4 counts as obesity if waist-to-height is 0.5 or more or waist is at least 80 cm (women) / 90 cm (men), otherwise overweight, and BMI 27.5 or more is obesity; with a non-Asian background the same applies at BMI 25-29.9 with 88 / 102 cm, and BMI 30 or more is obesity. ADA's "Asian background" means Asia (e.g., China, Japan, Korea) and the South Asian subcontinent, so the Black African and Middle Eastern groups use the non-Asian waist thresholds even though they get lower BMI cut-offs here (from NICE). For a percentage blend that mixes Asian and non-Asian backgrounds there is no ADA rule, so both classifications are shown when they differ. Waist never changes the suggested weight range.
  • Conditions: they do not move the weight range (apart from the diabetes buffer zone in step 4). If you are above the healthy range, the page shows the 5-7% weight-loss goal from diabetes-prevention guidelines (the Diabetes Prevention Program targeted at least 7%), plus condition-specific food notes.
  • Rounding: BMI is rounded to one decimal, and the category uses that rounded value (so 22.96 shows as 23.0 and is classed as 23.0). Weights are shown to the nearest pound and half kilogram.
  • Age: adults 18 and over only. From 65, BMI is less accurate (muscle and height are lost with age), so the ranges are rougher. No weight range is given during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
  • How the range is built, in order:
    1. Ancestry sets the healthy range (BMI 18.5 to the overweight cut-off; a mix is blended).
    2. Frame size (wrist, and elbow if given) adjusts Hamwi; the four formulas give a formula range.
    3. Suggested range = where the formula range overlaps the healthy range (if every formula is outside it, the nearest edge of the healthy range).
    4. Lower-cut-off ancestry: a buffer zone is shown from BMI 22 (blended for a mix) up to the cut-off. If type 2 diabetes / prediabetes / family history is selected, the top of the suggested range is kept below that zone (if the whole range is in or above the zone, the result is a ceiling: "at most" the weight just below it). The 18.5 floor never moves. Every step keeps a larger frame from getting a lower range than a smaller one.
  • Not covered: under-18s, pregnancy, eating disorders, and medical weight targets set by your own clinician, which take priority.

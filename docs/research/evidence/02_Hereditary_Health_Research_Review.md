# Research Team 2 — Hereditary Diseases, Nutritional Genetics, and Individual Dietary Responses
**Evidence review | Version 1.0 | 2026-11-01 | Research-only—not a diagnostic service**

## Executive conclusion

The clinically valuable expansion is **condition- and phenotype-informed nutrition**, not assigning diets from ancestry or consumer genetic variants. Some Mendelian diagnoses fundamentally change dietary safety (PAH deficiency, inherited fructose intolerance, fatty-acid oxidation disorders, and confirmed celiac disease). Other genetic findings justify diagnostic evaluation or cascade screening (familial hypercholesterolemia, hereditary hemochromatosis, selected monogenic diabetes) but do **not** supply a unique weight target or macronutrient percentage. Common variants (FTO, AMY1, FADS, APOA2) and T2D polygenic scores currently lack sufficiently replicated diet-treatment interactions to drive specific diets. Observed HbA1c, LDL-C/non-HDL-C/apoB, triglycerides, kidney function, blood pressure, symptoms, adherence, and sustained weight response are more actionable.

**Priority safety finding:** Do not recommend a ketogenic or fasting-based diet to a person with known/suspected fatty-acid oxidation defects. This is not a general warning against all carbohydrate restriction: it is a condition-specific contraindication/review flag [MCAD, LCHAD].

## Scope, methods, and evidence rules

- Searched authoritative clinical guidance, prospective cohorts, PubMed-indexed trials, systematic reviews, and GeneReviews available through **November 1, 2026**. This is a targeted structured review, **not** a preregistered, exhaustive systematic review, nor a guarantee that all later evidence has been captured. Where a paper was available as a PubMed abstract, conclusions are constrained to those data. No independent raw-data meta-analysis was performed.
- Prefer endpoints and randomized treatment interactions over correlations, generalizability over ancestry-specific convenience samples, and clinical utility over purely predictive association. Ancestry is not a proxy for a genotype, diet, diagnosis, or socioeconomic conditions.
- Matrix grades are **project-level judgments**, not formally conducted GRADE certainty: A = guideline/multiple robust trials for the *specific claim*; B = persuasive trial/cohort or high-quality guideline with some restrictions; C = limited/indirect evidence; D = unvalidated or conflicting. The five required `clinical_status` values are a **separate dimension**: guideline-supported, observational, exploratory, insufficient, contradicted. “Contradicted” refers to a specific prediction, never the existence of the genetic association.
- Keep distinguishable: (1) verified diagnosis; (2) pathogenic/likely-pathogenic variant interpreted clinically; (3) family history; (4) symptoms/abnormal labs without diagnosis; (5) common susceptibility variant or PRS; (6) ancestry-only prevalence context.
- Baseline comparator for patient-facing guidance = contemporary diet-quality/medical nutrition therapy guidance, **not** an unhealthy high-sugar diet.

## 1. Hereditary and genetic conditions: mechanism, penetrance, diagnostics, clinical action

### 1.1 Common type 2 diabetes and polygenic susceptibility — **risk is not diet selection**

T2D is a heterogeneous disease involving many common loci and the environment. Family history, prior gestational diabetes, excess visceral adiposity, age, activity, sleep and social determinants influence risk. Polygenic scores correlate with T2D incidence, but their calibration and discrimination vary across populations and settings. A 2025 multi-ancestry analysis (244,637 cases; 637,891 controls) reported an overall odds ratio of approximately **1.75 per SD** and **AUC 0.641** for a particular score; these are **not** treatment-effect estimates and do not show who should eat ketogenic. A separate 2026 large multi-ancestry evaluation reported incremental AUCs from roughly **0.02–0.16**, much lower in some underrepresented groups than European samples [PRS25, PRS26].

**Use:** family history and clinical risk may prompt guideline-concordant *screening*, clinician consultation, and behavior change. Use glucose/HbA1c for disease evaluation; interpret discordant HbA1c cautiously when hemoglobin variants, anemia or altered erythrocyte turnover are relevant. No ancestry-averaged PRS should determine a carbohydrate ceiling; no BMI equation changes from T2D PRS. Avoid presenting a PRS as a diagnosis.

### 1.2 Monogenic diabetes (GCK, HNF1A, HNF4A, HNF1B, neonatal forms) — **diagnostic pathway**

These are rare etiologies whose diagnosis materially alters clinical management. GCK-MODY frequently produces stable mild hyperglycemia rather than the progressive course of typical T2D; HNF1A/HNF4A disease may present young without typical insulin resistance; HNF1B can involve renal abnormalities. Genetic testing is considered when phenotype, family pattern, age, autoantibodies and C-peptide justify it. **Do not generalize T2D weight-loss targets or ketogenic advice to GCK-MODY.** Confirmed variants require qualified diabetes/genetics care; medication implications go to Team 3, including pregnancy exceptions [MODY]. High penetrance within affected families depends on gene/variant, age and presentation; no single reliable all-variant penetrance percentage is supplied here.

### 1.3 Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) — **screening, LDL management, not diet alone**

Rare pathogenic variants in *LDLR, APOB, PCSK9* and related genes can cause lifelong high LDL-C and high atherosclerotic risk; monogenic FH prevalence is approximately **1:250** in the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline. Severe untreated LDL-C (e.g., **≥190 mg/dL**) or family history of premature ASCVD/FH deserves clinical evaluation. Genetic testing can identify affected relatives via cascade screening; **a negative panel does not exclude clinical FH**, particularly in populations less well represented in reference databases. Penetrance is variant-dependent, age-dependent and modified by LDL exposure; avoid a single number [FH26].

**Nutrition:** emphasize replacement of saturated fat with unsaturated fat, fiber-rich foods and overall cardioprotective patterns. FH should trigger **medical referral and lipid evaluation** irrespective of BMI. Ketogenic patterns that raise LDL-C/apoB require reconsideration, especially in FH. Diet is adjunctive, not a substitute for indicated clinical management. Distinguish “high cholesterol” from confirmed FH and from non-FH hypertriglyceridemia [LIPID26].

### 1.4 Hereditary hemochromatosis (HFE) — **incomplete penetrance**

The pathogenic risk configuration is most often *HFE* C282Y **homozygosity**, not merely carrying one C282Y allele. Variants are more frequent in people of some Northern European ancestries, but ancestry alone cannot diagnose overload. Allen et al. followed 31,192 people of Northern European descent; among C282Y homozygotes, documented iron-overload-related disease was **28.4% in men (95% CI 18.8–40.2) and 1.2% in women (95% CI 0.03–6.5)** in its defined subset and follow-up: *cohort-specific penetrance*, not universal lifetime risk [HFE08]. Biochemical abnormality is more common than clinical organ disease.

**Diagnosis:** transferrin saturation and ferritin first (also consider inflammation/liver disease), then clinically interpreted HFE testing and appropriate further evaluation. EASL 2022 describes biochemical thresholds in C282Y homozygotes and more confirmation for other genotypes. **Treatment is not a restrictive diet**: iron removal (typically phlebotomy) is primary. For confirmed iron overload, avoid iron supplementation and high-dose supplemental vitamin C as advised; avoid raw shellfish and assess alcohol/liver risk. Do not prohibit iron-rich foods or needed supplements based solely on a Northern European background or carrier status [EASL22].

### 1.5 Celiac disease and HLA-DQ2/DQ8 — **strong risk association, weak positive prediction**

HLA-DQ2/DQ8-related haplotypes are carried by nearly all confirmed celiac patients but also many unaffected people. DQ2.5 occurs in ~20–30% of the general population in GeneReviews; presence is **not diagnostic**. Broad HLA negativity is useful to *exclude* celiac disease in appropriately tested situations (careful interpretation for less common HLA risk configurations). Family history increases pretest probability [CELIACGN, ACG23].

**Diagnosis:** in a person eating gluten, clinician-directed **tTG-IgA plus total IgA** and additional evaluation/biopsy as indicated. Do not begin gluten restriction before testing if celiac is being evaluated, except under appropriate specialist guidance. **Confirmed celiac disease**: lifelong gluten-free diet with dietitian support and monitoring for iron, folate, B12 and other deficiencies. **HLA-positive alone**: no gluten-free recommendation. Symptom improvement after avoiding gluten does not prove celiac disease [ACG23].

### 1.6 Lactase non-persistence, LCT/MCM6 regulatory variants — **symptoms over ancestry**

Lactase persistence has a well-studied genetic basis, with **multiple independently evolved variants** in different populations; a European-centered variant test does not fully represent African, Middle Eastern or mixed ancestry. Genotype may predict adult lactase production under a relevant allele assay; it does not necessarily predict symptom severity, secondary lactose malabsorption or the quantity tolerated. Milk symptoms also overlap with celiac, IBS and other disorders. A clinician may use elimination/rechallenge or a hydrogen breath test and symptom documentation [LACT].

**Action:** for *symptomatic* intolerance, consider lactose-free dairy, tolerated yogurt/hard cheeses or fortified substitutes. Do not automatically ban dairy for an ancestry group; avoid unnecessary calcium/protein loss. Differentiate primary genetic nonpersistence, secondary malabsorption and milk allergy.

### 1.7 Hereditary obesity syndromes — **red flags rather than genetic macro assignment**

Severe obesity starting **before age five**, marked hyperphagia, developmental abnormalities or an extreme familial pattern can suggest *MC4R*, *LEPR*, *POMC*, *PCSK1*, Bardet–Biedl syndrome and related disorders. The Endocrine Society recommends considering genetic evaluation in the appropriate pediatric phenotype, not blanket obesity gene testing for adults [ENDOOB]. Penetrance and phenotype vary by gene/variant; rare high-impact disorders should not be confused with common FTO association. A pathogenic finding may modify specialty care, but **does not justify recalculating a BMI target automatically**. Medication-specific implications: Team 3.

### 1.8 Gout and urate genetics — **clinically relevant diagnosis, non-actionable diet genotype**

Variants in urate transport such as *ABCG2* and *SLC2A9* influence serum urate and gout susceptibility; genetic associations do not validate an ancestry-specific purine allocation or make dietary advice a substitute for clinical therapy. ACR 2020 **conditionally** recommends limiting alcohol, purines and high-fructose corn syrup, and weight loss for patients with gout and overweight/obesity, with **low or very low certainty** for several lifestyle outcomes. It recommends against adding vitamin C supplements solely to treat gout. Do not apply gout diet restrictions to a person with only elevated urate or family history without context [ACR20].

### 1.9 Other inherited metabolic conditions with genuinely specific nutrition consequences

| Disorder | Genetic / diagnostic distinction | Important diet or safety implication | Calculator handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency / PKU (*PAH*) | Biallelic disease; newborn screening, blood Phe, confirmatory evaluation | Lifelong Phe-focused medical nutrition therapy when untreated Phe >360 μmol/L per ACMG; metabolic-dietitian management | Hard specialist referral; do not generate normal high-protein keto plans [PKU24] |
| MCAD deficiency (*ACADM*) | Biallelic disease; acylcarnitines/genetic/enzymatic testing | **Avoid fasting and high-fat/low-carb diets**, risk of hypoketotic hypoglycemia and serious decompensation | High-priority safety block/referral [MCAD] |
| Long-chain FAO / trifunctional protein deficiencies (*HADHA/HADHB*) | Biallelic; biochemical/genetic specialist diagnosis | Avoid fasting and unsupervised high-fat ketogenic diets; disease-specific prescribed fat strategy | High-priority safety block/referral [LCHAD] |
| Hereditary fructose intolerance (*ALDOB*) | Biallelic; unlike ordinary fructose malabsorption | Severe reactions to fructose/sucrose/sorbitol; specialist-directed lifelong avoidance; do not perform fructose challenge | Hard specialist referral; diet restrictions only when verified or strongly suspected in urgent clinical setting [HFI] |
| Primary carnitine / other FAO disorders | Multiple genes, often recessive | Risk from fasting or inappropriate fat strategies; diagnosis-specific | Refer to metabolic genetics, not general keto advice [MCAD] |

Other candidate disorders for a later specialist-only appendix: classic galactosemia, glycogen storage diseases, urea-cycle disorders, Wilson disease, hereditary pancreatitis and some nutrient transport defects. **No detailed unsupervised nutritional algorithm is justified from this review for these conditions.** Severe inherited metabolic disorders can involve childhood and reproductive care that is outside the present adult-weight-calculator scope.

## 2. Dietary patterns and genetically predicted response — evidence hierarchy

**Negative controls are as important as positive leads.** DIETFITS (n=609, 12 months) reported healthy low-fat vs healthy low-carbohydrate weight losses **−5.3 vs −6.0 kg**, between-arm difference **0.7 kg (95% CI −0.2 to 1.6)**; genotype panel interaction **p=0.20** and 30-minute insulin secretion interaction **p=0.47**. These results contradict the *tested* simple matching algorithm, **not all conceivable nutrigenetics**. The low-carbohydrate arm averaged about 30% energy from carbohydrate at 12 months: it **was not a long-term randomized test of sustained nutritional ketosis** [DIETFITS].

An individual-participant meta-analysis of eight RCTs (n=9,563) found **no reliable FTO-variant modification** of weight-loss outcomes [FTO]. A 2025 secondary DIETFITS subgroup analysis reported an *APOA2*–saturated-fat interaction under particular sustained-intake conditions; this is intriguing but requires replication, broad ancestry testing, prespecified treatment-interaction trials and external validation before clinical matching [APOA2]. A 2026 systematic review of personalized-nutrition RCTs found **small weight/fat advantages** overall but no consistent improvements in BMI, waist, lipids or glycemic indices; its interventions were heterogeneous and not all genetically based [PN26].

**Carbohydrate restriction:** ADA 2026 allows individualized reduction in carbohydrates for some adults with diabetes, while emphasizing food quality, high fiber and medical safety. A systematic review of 23 RCTs (n=1,357) reported more HbA1c-defined remission at six months for low-carb diets under one definition (**57% vs 31%; reported RD 0.32, 95% CI 0.17–0.47**); the signal weakened when remission excluded medication use and by 12 months [BMJLCD]. The randomized 12-week crossover Keto–Med trial (40 enrolled; 33 with complete primary data) found **no significant HbA1c advantage** of ketogenic over a Mediterranean-plus pattern, more triglyceride lowering (**−16% vs −5%**) but an LDL increase (**+10% vs −5%**) [KETOMED]. **Neither trial validates a genotype-based keto prescription.**

**Mediterranean and DASH:** these patterns have strong outcomes evidence without genotype matching. The reanalyzed PREDIMED primary-prevention study (n=7,447; design irregularities corrected through reanalysis) estimated hazard ratios for major cardiovascular events **0.69 (0.53–0.91)** with olive oil and **0.72 (0.54–0.95)** with nuts vs control [PREDIMED]. CORDIOPREV enrolled 1,002 adults with established coronary disease; Mediterranean vs low-fat diet yielded adjusted HRs **0.719–0.753** for composite major cardiovascular events over seven years [CORDIO]. DASH-Sodium lowered systolic BP vs high-sodium control by **11.5 mm Hg in participants with hypertension** under its controlled feeding conditions [DASHSALT]. These populations and comparators are not interchangeable with every general-population user.

**Fat composition:** 2026 dyslipidemia guidance favors predominantly plant-based Mediterranean/DASH/vegetarian patterns for LDL and clinical ASCVD risk; it acknowledges that ketogenic diets can raise LDL markedly in some people. Monitor LDL-C, preferably non-HDL-C/apoB when indicated, rather than inferring cardiovascular protection from lower triglycerides or higher HDL alone [LIPID26]. An RCT systematic review of saturated-fat modification reported uncertain mortality and event estimates with several confidence intervals crossing null: avoid the unsupported absolute claim that any amount of saturated fat reduction has a guaranteed event benefit for every patient [SAT26].

**AMY1/FADS2:** population-specific AMY1 copy distributions and FADS variants help explain evolutionary biology; they are not validated diet-matching prescriptions. A 2026 AMY1 review explicitly reports conflicting adiposity associations and insufficient clinical predictive accuracy [AMY26]. Treat FADS2→omega-3 conversion as a mechanistic lead, not evidence to deprive an ancestry group of plant foods or prescribe supplements.

## 3. Risk of harm and conflicting conditions

1. **Known FAO defect + keto/fasting:** potentially life-threatening; hard stop and metabolic specialist referral [MCAD, LCHAD].
2. **Diabetes + SGLT2 inhibitor + ketogenic eating:** DKA risk; ADA advises discouraging the ketogenic pattern and appropriate medical assessment. **Team 3 owns medication details** [ADA26].
3. **Diabetes + insulin/other glucose-lowering therapy + major carb reduction:** hypoglycemia risk and possible treatment adjustment by prescriber. Team 3 owns drug specifics [ADA26].
4. **High LDL/FH + high saturated-fat keto:** potentially worse atherogenic burden; recheck full lipids and clinician review [LIPID26].
5. **Pregnancy/lactation, children, CKD, eating-disorder risk:** ADA does not recommend very-low-carbohydrate diets as general advice in these contexts [ADA26].
6. **Celiac suspicion + stopping gluten before tests:** can obscure diagnosis [ACG23].
7. **Iron deficiency + ancestry-only hemochromatosis warning:** can be harmed by unwarranted supplement avoidance. Base decisions on testing and clinician oversight [EASL22].
8. **Gout + ultra-high-purine/high-fructose choices:** gout flares or urate worsening; conditional evidence and individual response rather than universal bans [ACR20].
9. **Coexisting kidney disease and high-protein diets**, malnutrition, food insecurity, or restrictive-eating history: need clinician/RD tailoring, not one-size macros.
10. **Familial hypercholesterolemia + weight-only target:** dangerously false reassurance; a normal BMI does not negate inherited LDL risk.

## 4. Ancestry and penetrance: interpretive table

| Finding | What ancestry may contribute | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| HFE C282Y | Pretest prevalence differs; family history and TSAT/ferritin improve triage | Iron overload or need for iron restriction based on European ancestry |
| Lactase persistence | Frequencies of different LCT-region variants | An individual's symptoms or dairy tolerance |
| Celiac-associated HLA | Haplotype frequency/context | Confirmed celiac diagnosis or need for gluten-free diet |
| Common T2D loci / PRS | May influence population risk and model transportability | Best diet, weight target, actual T2D diagnosis |
| MC4R/POMC/LEPR and other rare variants | Founder effects in certain populations may inform genetic evaluation | A syndrome from appearance or ethnic identity |
| AMY1/FADS2 | Ancestry-related allele/copy frequencies | Grams of starch, fat, or supplements an individual needs |

Clinical penetrance must be **phenotype-, population-, age-, sex- and variant-specific**; unknown is preferable to a made-up point estimate.

## 5. Recommended action taxonomy

- **SCREENING:** severe LDL / FH family history; compatible iron indices; likely celiac symptoms/family history; early-onset non-typical diabetes; extreme pediatric-onset hyperphagia; suggest relevant *clinician evaluation* rather than ordering genetic tests automatically.
- **PATIENT EDUCATION:** general healthy patterns; lactose symptom management; family-history awareness; contextual explanation of inherited susceptibility and uncertainty.
- **DIETARY INTERVENTION:** diagnosed celiac, PKU, HFI, specialist-managed FAO defects, phenotype-led cardiometabolic diet modification with monitoring.
- **RESEARCH ONLY:** FTO/AMY1/FADS2/APOA2 diet matching, T2D PRS-guided keto selection, ancestry-specific carbohydrate ceilings.

## 7. Limitations and decisive unanswered questions

- Penetrance estimates often reflect selected populations, ascertainment, ages and changing diagnostic definitions; examples must not become personal forecasts.
- Data from intervention trials often include high-intensity counseling, small diet contrasts in real life, short follow-up, adherence selection, varying keto definitions and limited demographic representation.
- Genetic tests on consumer arrays have varying allele coverage; pathogenicity requires clinical-grade variant interpretation, zygosity, laboratory quality and variant classification updates; VUS must not trigger diet.
- PRS portability and measurement fairness across admixed populations remain unresolved; evaluation must calibrate models per clinical outcome and subgroup, not simply compare frequency differences.
- Hard long-term cardiovascular outcomes for sustained nutritional ketosis, particularly in people with preexisting severe LDL abnormalities, remain insufficient to claim equivalence or superiority to Mediterranean/DASH.
- The updated 2026 personalized-nutrition meta-analysis is **not specifically a validation of genetics-driven nutrition** and must not be used as such.

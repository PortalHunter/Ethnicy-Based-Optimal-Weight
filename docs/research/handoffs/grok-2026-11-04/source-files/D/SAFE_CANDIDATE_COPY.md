## Grok Team D — Candidate Public-Facing Educational Statements
**Principles**: Research-based patterns for discussion with clinicians. No diagnoses, prescriptions, drug changes, genotype inference, ideal-weight equations, or automatic personal outcomes. Distinguish short-term surrogates from hard clinical events. Note uncertainties and coexisting marker changes (e.g., triglycerides down with LDL up). “No definitive evidence” is not the same as “proven false.”

### Mediterranean-style patterns
In a large Spanish trial, Mediterranean-style diets that included extra-virgin olive oil or nuts were associated with fewer major cardiovascular events than a control diet that advised reducing fat. The original 2013 report was retracted and republished in 2018 after problems with how some participants were assigned to diets; the main finding remained similar after reanalysis. This supports discussion of Mediterranean-style eating patterns that emphasize vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, fish, and olive oil, while limiting refined grains and added sugars. It does not prove that every version labeled “Mediterranean” produces the same result, nor does it replace individualized medical care.

### DASH and sodium
In a controlled feeding study, the DASH eating pattern (rich in fruits, vegetables, and low-fat dairy, lower in saturated fat) combined with lower sodium intake reduced blood pressure more than either change by itself. The reductions were larger in people who started with higher blood pressure. These are short-term findings under supervised conditions.

### Portfolio-style pattern
Controlled trials of a dietary pattern that combines nuts, plant protein (such as soy or legumes), viscous fiber, and plant sterols have shown average reductions in LDL cholesterol of about 17% beyond a standard low-saturated-fat diet. The size of the change depends on how closely the pattern is followed. These are laboratory lipid results from relatively short studies.

### Low-carbohydrate and ketogenic approaches
Short-term randomized trials show that well-formulated lower-carbohydrate and ketogenic patterns can improve triglycerides, weight, and blood glucose measures in some adults with overweight or type 2 diabetes. The same trials frequently show increases in LDL cholesterol. In one 12-week crossover comparison of a well-formulated ketogenic diet and a Mediterranean-style diet (both limiting added sugars and refined grains), HbA1c improved similarly on both; the ketogenic diet lowered triglycerides more but raised LDL cholesterol and appeared less sustainable afterward.  

Meta-analyses of randomized trials confirm the pattern of favorable changes in triglycerides and weight alongside rises in LDL and total cholesterol. No large randomized trial has demonstrated that ketogenic diets reduce heart attacks, strokes, or cardiovascular death. Current guidelines note that people with diabetes who take SGLT2 inhibitors and are at risk for ketoacidosis should be educated about that risk, given tools to measure ketones, and discouraged from a ketogenic eating pattern.

### Genotype and diet matching
In the DIETFITS trial of 609 overweight adults, a healthy low-fat diet and a healthy low-carbohydrate diet produced similar average weight loss after one year. A pre-specified genetic pattern and a measure of insulin secretion did not predict who would do better on one diet versus the other. Claims that a simple genetic test can identify the “best” macronutrient ratio for an individual are not supported by this well-powered trial.

### General caveats for any pattern
- Improvements in one laboratory marker (for example, lower triglycerides or HbA1c) can occur together with changes in another marker that may be unfavorable (for example, higher LDL cholesterol).
- Short-term changes in blood tests or weight do not automatically translate into fewer heart attacks or longer life.
- Observational studies and Mendelian randomization analyses provide supporting or contradictory context but have their own limitations (confounding, pleiotropy, ancestry specificity).
- No dietary pattern should be presented as proven superior for every person or every demographic group in the absence of validated patient-level evidence.
- All statements are for educational discussion with a qualified clinician. They are not personal medical advice, diagnoses, or treatment recommendations.

**Recommended dispositions**: The above statements are suitable for educational use with the stated caveats. Claims of genotype-based diet matching for the DIETFITS panel, unqualified long-term cardiovascular superiority of ketogenic diets, or ideal-weight calculations derived from ancestry should be withheld.

### claim_id: KETOMED_2022_HBA1C
**exact_claim**: In adults with prediabetes or T2DM, 12-week well-formulated ketogenic diet (WFKD) and Mediterranean-plus diet (Med-Plus) produced similar percentage reductions in HbA1c with no significant between-diet difference.  
**diet_definition**: WFKD (very low carbohydrate, emphasizing non-starchy vegetables, limited high-protein, avoiding legumes/fruits/whole intact grains) vs Med-Plus (incorporating legumes, fruits, whole intact grains; both diets limited added sugars and refined grains).  
**population_definition**: Adults ≥18 years with prediabetes (HbA1c 5.7–6.4% or fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL) or T2DM; primary analysis n=33 with complete data (from ~40 enrolled).  
**study_type**: Randomized crossover interventional trial (free-living with food delivery support).  
**comparison**: WFKD vs Med-Plus, each 12 weeks, random order.  
**sample_size**: 33 (complete-case primary analysis).  
**followup**: 12 weeks per phase.  
**outcome**: Percentage change in HbA1c (primary); secondary lipids, weight, nutrients.  
**effect_size_and_CI**: HbA1c mean % change WFKD −9% (95% CI −11% to −7%), Med-Plus −7% (95% CI −9% to −5%); between-diet P=0.11. Triglycerides: −16% (SEM 4%) vs −5% (SEM 6%), P=0.02. LDL-C: +10% (SEM 4%) vs −5% (SEM 5%), P=0.01. Weight: ~8% vs ~7% (order interaction noted).  
**p_value_if_relevant**: Primary between-diet P=0.11; TG P=0.02; LDL-C P=0.01.  
**primary_DOI_PMID_URL**: DOI 10.1093/ajcn/nqac154; PMID 35641199. Correction: DOI 10.1093/ajcn/nqac279 (Am J Clin Nutr 2022;116:1904).  
**errata_status**: November/December 2022 (published ~Dec 2022 / Jan 2023) Figure 3 y-axis correction only (from −0.4 to 0.2 to −40 to 20) to clarify percentage scale. No changes to data points, statistics, or conclusions.  
**risk_of_bias**: Small sample; short duration; crossover with possible carryover; free-living adherence variable; COVID-era self-reported weights in some; order interactions for some outcomes.  
**quality_of_evidence**: Moderate for short-term surrogate markers in this specific comparison; low for long-term or hard clinical outcomes.  
**limits_and_harms**: LDL-C rise on WFKD; lower fiber and several micronutrients; lower sustainability in follow-up. Surrogate (HbA1c, lipids) improvements do not establish hard CVD or mortality benefit.  
**transportability**: Limited to similar free-living adults with prediabetes/T2DM willing to follow structured delivery-supported diets; not generalizable to unsupervised long-term use or other populations.  
**source_verified_on**: 2026-11-04 (primary paper and correction abstracts/full descriptions retrieved).  
**app_safe_statement**: In a small 12-week crossover trial of adults with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, a well-formulated ketogenic diet and a Mediterranean-style diet that both limited added sugars and refined grains produced similar short-term improvements in HbA1c. The ketogenic approach lowered triglycerides more but raised LDL cholesterol and was less sustainable in follow-up. These are short-term laboratory findings, not proof of long-term clinical benefit or safety.  
**no_personal_inference**: True.  
**cross_team_dependencies**: None assumed; independent verification.  
**recommended_disposition**: educational (context_only for any superiority claim).

### claim_id: PREDIMED_2018_REPUB
**exact_claim**: Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts reduced composite major cardiovascular events by approximately 30% relative to a control diet advising reduced fat, in high-risk adults.  
**diet_definition**: Med + EVOO or Med + nuts vs control (advice to reduce dietary fat). Not a pure “Mediterranean pattern” comparison; intervention included free provision of oil or nuts.  
**population_definition**: Adults at high cardiovascular risk (Spain), original n=7447; reanalysis addressed protocol deviations affecting a substantial minority.  
**study_type**: Multicenter randomized trial (with documented randomization protocol deviations).  
**comparison**: Two Med-supplemented arms vs low-fat advice control.  
**sample_size**: Original 7447; reanalysis omitted or adjusted for ~1588 (or ~14–21% depending on source) with household or clinic-level allocation issues.  
**followup**: Median ~4.8 years.  
**outcome**: Composite of MI, stroke, or cardiovascular death (hard clinical events; driven largely by stroke).  
**effect_size_and_CI**: Republished analyses retained roughly similar hazard ratios (~0.69–0.72 range reported in secondary summaries; primary effect size essentially unchanged after adjustment). Exact reanalysis CIs require full text.  
**p_value_if_relevant**: Remained statistically significant in reanalysis.  
**primary_DOI_PMID_URL**: Original 2013 retracted; republished N Engl J Med 2018;378:e34 (and related). See Nutrition Source summary and NEJM editor notes.  
**errata_status**: Full retraction of 2013 paper and republication in 2018 after identification of randomization protocol deviations (households assigned same diet; some clinic-level rather than individual randomization). Results direction and approximate magnitude preserved after statistical correction and sensitivity analyses.  
**risk_of_bias**: Protocol deviations in randomization for a non-trivial fraction; composite endpoint; control not strictly non-Mediterranean; provision of supplements; generalizability outside the trial setting.  
**quality_of_evidence**: Moderate for the specific supplemented Mediterranean approach in high-risk Spanish adults after reanalysis; residual uncertainty from protocol issues.  
**limits_and_harms**: Not proof that any “Mediterranean” label is superior; effect concentrated in stroke; no clear total mortality reduction in primary report.  
**transportability**: High-risk adults in Spain under trial conditions; less clear for other populations or unsupervised patterns.  
**source_verified_on**: 2026-11-04 (multiple secondary descriptions of retraction/republication and author statements; full republished paper not fully re-extracted here).  
**app_safe_statement**: A large Spanish trial that tested Mediterranean-style diets supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts found fewer major cardiovascular events than a control diet that advised reducing fat. The original 2013 report was retracted and republished in 2018 after randomization problems were identified; the main finding remained similar after reanalysis. This is evidence for the specific trial diets, not a blanket recommendation.  
**no_personal_inference**: True.  
**cross_team_dependencies**: None.  
**recommended_disposition**: educational (with explicit note of 2018 retraction/republication).

**diet_definition**: DASH (fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy, reduced saturated fat) vs typical American control diet; sodium levels high (~3300 mg), intermediate (~2300 mg), low (~1500 mg) at 2100 kcal.  
**population_definition**: Adults with SBP 120–159 mm Hg and DBP 80–95 mm Hg, not on antihypertensives; n=412.  
**study_type**: Controlled feeding randomized trial (crossover sodium levels within parallel diet arms).

**sample_size**: 412.

**outcome**: Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (surrogate).

**p_value_if_relevant**: Significant reductions reported (P<0.001 for many contrasts).

### Additional notes on hard vs surrogate outcomes and contrary evidence
Ketogenic approaches consistently show favorable short-term changes in triglycerides, weight, and glycemia alongside increases in LDL-C and total cholesterol in meta-analyses of RCTs (e.g., ~0.35 mmol/L LDL-C increase). No large RCTs demonstrate reduction in hard cardiovascular events with ketogenic diets; observational and MR data support LDL-C (or apoB) as causal for atherosclerotic disease. Mediterranean and DASH patterns have more direct trial support for BP or composite events under study conditions.  

**Mendelian randomization**: Supports causal role of LDL-C/apoB in coronary disease; weaker or null for some stroke subtypes. Limitations include residual pleiotropy, population specificity (often European ancestry), and inability to capture diet-quality nuances.  

**Observational epidemiology**: Subject to residual confounding and reverse causation; useful for hypothesis generation but inferior to well-conducted RCTs for causal claims about diet.  

No demographic subgroup recommendations are supported without validated patient-level predictive evidence (DIETFITS null result is illustrative).  

**Claims recommended for removal or down-rating**: Any assertion that a specific genotype dictates optimal macronutrient ratio (for the tested DIETFITS panel); claims that short-term TG or HbA1c improvement on keto equates to net cardiovascular benefit without addressing LDL-C rise; unqualified “Mediterranean is proven superior” without noting PREDIMED protocol issues and the specific supplemented intervention.

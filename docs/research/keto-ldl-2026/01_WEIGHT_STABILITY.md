# 01 — Cholesterol during weight loss, stable scale weight and body recomposition

**Question:** Is persistently high LDL-C after eight-plus weeks at unchanged *scale* weight explained by ongoing fat loss and adipose cholesterol mobilization, particularly if lean tissue is increasing? **Answer:** It is physically possible that fat mass is still falling; no direct human evidence establishes that this explains persistent LDL-C elevation or specifies a post-plateau time-to-normalization. Evidence is highly indirect. Sources below map to [SOURCE_LEDGER.md](SOURCE_LEDGER.md).

## A. Biological compartments: avoid false equivalence

- **Lipolysis** is adipose triglyceride breakdown into glycerol and fatty acids; basal lipolysis occurs even at net energy balance. Net adipose change depends on uptake, re-esterification, and oxidation across days.
- **Cholesterol in adipose** exists in cell membranes and associated lipid pools. Adipose cholesterol homeostasis does not obey the same flux law as stored triglyceride. A reduction in fat-droplet triglyceride mass does *not* imply proportionate serum cholesterol release. [S01, S02]
- **Adipocyte-to-blood cholesterol transfer** could involve transfer to HDL and downstream hepatic processing; it is not equivalent to direct conversion of fat-cell cholesterol into new LDL particles. The liver produces VLDL with one apoB-100 per particle; metabolism converts fractions of VLDL/IDL to LDL and clearance removes particles via LDL receptor-dependent and other pathways.
- **LDL-C is a stock variable**, roughly the amount of cholesterol carried in circulating LDL at measurement time. It is not a direct measure of the flux of cholesterol leaving adipocytes. The stock can rise from greater particle production, longer circulation time, more cholesterol cargo per particle, or combinations.
- **ApoB estimates a different stock**—the number of atherogenic particles—so simultaneous high apoB suggests a particle-number process in addition to or instead of simple cholesterol enrichment. Human kinetic measurements would be needed to allocate cause. [S04, S18, S19]

### Three genuinely different forms of “plateau”

| State | Scale mass | Fat mass | Lean mass/water | What can be inferred |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Ordinary plateau | Approximately unchanged | Unknown | Unknown | No confirmation that adipose loss ceased; no evidence of cholesterol release |
| B. Recomposition | Approximately unchanged | Measurably decreasing | Increasing offset | Fat loss persists; the LDL consequence remains unmeasured |
| C. Confirmed maintenance | Approximately unchanged | Stable by repeated quality body-composition measures | Stable within measurement precision | Continued adipose-loss hypothesis becomes less likely, but no proven cut-point rules out minor flux |

Note that DXA measures lean soft tissue, not muscle protein alone. Glycogen, extracellular fluid and measurement reproducibility affect apparent gains. Repeat scans with standardized hydration, exercise and timing; MRI can clarify adipose compartments in research.

## B. Direct weight-loss evidence and contradictory tests

**[S01] Phinney et al. 1991 (n=6):** Six women underwent *very-low-calorie* weight loss, not a specified weight-stable ketogenic recomposition program. Total cholesterol initially declined during weight loss, rebounded as weight loss continued, and then declined from 5.95 to 4.92 mmol/L after ≥2 months in maintenance. The study sampled adipose cholesterol; abdominal adipose cholesterol content increased while peripheral biopsy sites did not show an equivalent change. Its interpretation was “possibly” from mobilization, not proof of cholesterol tracer flux into LDL. Critically, the observed maintenance trajectory argues against using this study to assert LDL elevation must last *beyond* eight weeks. [S01]

**[S02] Schaefer et al. 1983 (n=3):** Three people on weight-reduction formula diets lost substantial weight over 11–25 weeks. Adipocyte triglyceride mass dropped about 40%, yet adipocyte cholesterol per cell and plasma cholesterol did not change materially. The tissue sample was from buttock fat, not all depots, and sample size was tiny. But the study directly refutes the assumption that triglyceride loss guarantees concomitant efflux of cholesterol. [S02]

**[S03] Wadden et al. 1999 (n=25):** A longer observational series found marked early LDL decreases followed by attenuated LDL benefit at later time points even after additional weight loss. Importantly, women regained an average 7.4 kg from week 48 to 100 while remaining at least 5% below original weight. “Maintained weight loss” thus did *not* mean 52 consecutive weeks of stable weight. This cannot establish cholesterol mobilization for a fixed 8-, 12-, or 24-week interval. [S03]

**[S21] DIETFITS DXA secondary study (2026):** DXA at baseline, six and twelve months in 323 diet-induced weight losers documents that fat and lean compartments change independently. In that sample many regained fat mass later; it does not provide LDL-apoB kinetics, a ketogenic sample, or controlled weight-stable recomposition. The relevance is measurement methodology, not evidence for cholesterol release. [S21]

## C. Weight-stable ketogenic counterevidence and practical interpretation

**[S26] Retterstøl et al. (2018):** In a randomized parallel study, 15 healthy normal-weight adults on <20 g/day carbohydrate for three weeks and 15 habitual-diet controls completed follow-up. LDL-C rose from 2.2 to 3.1 mmol/L in the LCHF arm (reported 44% relative change versus controls), and apoB rose significantly, while **body weight and body composition did not differ significantly between groups**. The trial is short and small, and the intervention increased saturated-fat exposure substantially. Nonetheless it directly undermines a claim that active or residual adipose fat loss is *necessary* for a large low-carbohydrate LDL-C response. It cannot answer how long cholesterol release persists after a former weight-loss plateau. [S26]

**[S04] Burén et al.:** A randomized four-week ketogenic *feeding* intervention in 17 normal-weight women increased LDL-C by 1.82 mmol/L and apoB, with both large and small LDL cholesterol fractions increasing. The study attempted weight maintenance and adjusted for small weight differences; it demonstrates that severe weight loss is not required for an LDL/apoB response to a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet. These data are not a test of adipose cholesterol release per se. [S04]

**[S08] Crosier and McPherson:** Two cases reported stable body weight for years while consuming high-saturated-fat ketogenic diets and had substantially elevated LDL-C, which fell after diet modification. No serial DXA was obtained, and carbohydrate, saturated fat and cholesterol intake changed together. Nevertheless, prolonged stable *weight* does not preclude diet-related hypercholesterolemia. [S08]

**[S07] CARBFUNC:** In adults with baseline obesity, two-year LCHF had low TG/remnants, higher LDL-C (particularly large LDL) and no statistically significant within-arm apoB rise. The substantial dropout and weight changes preclude claiming this establishes a maintenance-specific mechanism. It does show potential LDL-C/apoB heterogeneity. [S07]

## D. Is there an evidence-based eight-week time constant?

**No.** The following scenarios should be separated:

1. **Exactly eight weeks of stable scale weight, fat mass unknown:** neither a continuing adipose-loss process nor its absence can be established.
2. **Eight weeks of stable scale weight, verified fat mass still decreasing:** ongoing net adipose tissue loss is supported; whether cholesterol flux is enough to affect LDL-C is untested.
3. **Eight or more weeks of verified *weight and fat-mass* stability:** a transient adipose-loss-only theory becomes less credible; persistent dietary composition effects, hepatic secretion/clearance adaptation, genetic predisposition, secondary diseases and medications remain possible.
4. **Eight or more weeks and high LDL-C plus apoB:** clinically meaningful particle concentration may be elevated regardless of causal attribution. It is inappropriate to infer low risk from TG/HDL alone. [S19, S20]

A conventional 4–12-week clinical interval for repeating lipids under a stable intervention is a useful monitoring convention, **not** a biologically validated interval after which adipose cholesterol has certainly washed out. LDL particle residence time is not the same as the time course of hepatic metabolic adaptation, stored cholesterol turnover, or body-recomposition physiology.

## E. Competing causal hypotheses and testable predictions

| Hypothesis | Predicted biomarker change or distinct test | Evidence level |
|---|---|---|
| H1: shrinking adipose cholesterol stores transiently contribute to plasma cholesterol | High net adipose cholesterol export on tracer assay; LDL-C decline when *fat-mass* loss ceases, holding diet constant; apoB may or may not change | Plausible, **not directly shown after stable scale weight** [S01, S02] |
| H2: saturated-fat/dietary-cholesterol change affects hepatic LDL clearance | Diet-fat substitution with unchanged calories and carbohydrate changes LDL-C and often apoB; LDL fractional catabolic rate may change | Stronger general diet biology and weight-stable intervention consistency [S04, S08, S20] |
| H3: carbohydrate restriction shifts VLDL secretion, TG uptake and conversion to cholesterol-rich LDL | Simultaneous VLDL-TG/apoB kinetic measurements plus LDL particle conversion and catabolic rate across controlled carb conditions | Mechanistic hypothesis needing direct LMHR tracer confirmation [S12] |
| H4: genetic/secondary cause contributes to clearance impairment | High LDL-C before keto, family history, thyroid/renal findings, persistent response despite substantial diet alteration, variants in FH genes where clinically indicated | Differential diagnosis rather than universal genotype-based diet assignment [S20] |
| H5: cargo enrichment without marked particle-count increase | Higher LDL-C relative to apoB/LDL-P, with higher average LDL cholesterol per particle | Observed in some cohort interventions, but does not rule out LDL-related risk [S06, S07, S19] |

A dietary n-of-1 perturbation can discriminate **responsive versus nonresponsive biomarker patterns** but cannot, alone, identify which hepatic kinetic pathway caused the change.

## F. Target protocol to answer the actual recomposition question

**Population:** adults recently completing ≥10% weight loss, with oversampling of genuinely lean keto-associated LDL responders; a parallel carbohydrate-matched nonketogenic control to separate dietary composition.

**Require three arms/states rather than a single “plateau” definition:** ongoing weight loss; stable scale weight with confirmed DXA/MRI fat loss and lean gain; stable weight *and* stable fat mass. Prospectively stratify for ketogenic exposure and saturated fat.

**Timeline:** at least 4-week diet/baseline run-in, then planned measurements at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16 and 24 weeks after the onset of a verified plateau. Prespecify error bounds for weight trends and DXA change exceeding technical measurement error; standardize diet energy, activity, hydration, drugs and time since last exercise.

**Measurements:** exact diet composition and fiber; morning serial weight; waist, DXA/MRI; fasting/nonfasting routine lipids; apoB, Lp(a), non-HDL-C, NMR LDL-P and subclasses; thyroid/kidney/liver context; plasma fatty-acid/glycerol turnover and VLDL/LDL apoB production/catabolic rate via labeled tracers; adipose cholesterol distribution if ethically feasible. Pre-register analysis of *within-person* LDL/apoB change after measured fat loss ceases, rather than selecting “responders” post hoc.

**Primary contrast:** LDL-C and apoB change in documented recomposition versus confirmed fat-mass-stable maintenance with matched diet and energy intake, adjusting for baseline BMI and lipids.

**What would falsify the strong mobilization claim?** Reproducibly high LDL-C/apoB despite confirmed stable fat mass, independent of weight trajectory, with rapid reduction on an isocaloric dietary fat swap—or tracer data inconsistent with substantial adipose cholesterol delivery. Conversely, a fall precisely aligned with cessation of objectively documented fat loss under unchanged diet would support, but not by itself prove, adipose-derived cholesterol as the cause.

## Bottom line

**After ≥8 weeks of unchanged scale weight there is no evidence-based presumption that excess LDL-C is still “leftover cholesterol from fat burning.”** Continued fat loss is possible but requires measurement. The best available weight-loss evidence is tiny and partly contradictory, while randomized short trials and stable-weight case data show that an ongoing dietary effect can exist without substantial weight loss.

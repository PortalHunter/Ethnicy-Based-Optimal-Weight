# 04 — LMHR phenotype, dietary fat, weight-stable trials and coronary imaging

**Evidence cut-off:** 2026-11-05. See [source ledger](SOURCE_LEDGER.md) for study identifiers, original publications, and critical correction/retraction status.

## 1. What the LMHR phenotype actually means

The operational *lean mass hyper-responder* (LMHR) definition proposed in a self-selected survey is **LDL-C ≥200 mg/dL, HDL-C ≥80 mg/dL and TG ≤70 mg/dL** on carbohydrate restriction. It is a research description, **not a validated clinical diagnostic entity**, cardiovascular-risk classification, or treatment exemption. [S11]

A survey of 548 self-selected respondents reported that low baseline BMI and a lower TG/HDL ratio predicted larger LDL-C rises; selection, recalled diet, nonrandomized exposure and unverified pre-post laboratory results limit causal and population estimates. [S11] A 2024 meta-analysis of 41 randomized low-carbohydrate trials (n=1,379, mean intervention ~19.4 weeks) independently found an inverse association between study mean baseline BMI and LDL-C change, with the leanest BMI subgroup having an average LDL-C rise of approximately 41 mg/dL. This subgroup average does **not** establish how often LDL-C ≥200 mg/dL occurs, how an individual will respond, or which molecule drives the response. [S10]

**Related but distinct phenotypes**: Some ketogenic dieters have severe LDL-C increases but do not have HDL-C ≥80 mg/dL or TG ≤70 mg/dL, and some had higher BMI at initiation. Three September 2026 cases (published online; indexed October) had LDL-C peaks 364–682 mg/dL and did not consistently satisfy LMHR thresholds. Do not restrict screening to “classical LMHR” features. [S09]

## 2. Mechanistic models and direct observations

### Lipid energy model: a hypothesis, not proof

The proposed lipid energy model (LEM) suggests greater peripheral reliance on fatty-acid fuel with carbohydrate restriction, increased hepatic secretion of VLDL-TG, rapid peripheral TG removal by lipoprotein lipase, greater VLDL-to-LDL conversion, cholesterol-rich LDL accumulation, and HDL remodeling. It could account qualitatively for the triad of low TG, high HDL and high LDL in some lean people. [S12]

**Unresolved kinetic tests:** The model implies specific changes in the *production rate* of VLDL-TG and VLDL-apoB, the rate of VLDL→IDL→LDL conversion, lipoprotein-lipase activity, LDL-apoB fractional catabolic rate and cholesterol content per LDL particle. Cross-sectional values of LDL-C, TG and HDL-C **cannot** identify these fluxes. A low fasting TG value, for instance, does not itself prove increased VLDL secretion; low TG can reflect lower secretion, faster clearance or both. An observed high LDL-C could result from impaired clearance rather than increased conversion alone.

**Distinct hypothesis:** Liberation of cholesterol from shrinking adipocytes during active fat loss is *not* the same as LEM. They can coexist in principle, but neither has demonstrated a persistent ≥8-week weight-plateau contribution to LDL-C in LMHR using direct isotopic measurement. [S01, S02, S12]

### LDL receptor / saturated fat / genetics

A high saturated-fat diet can lower the clearance of LDL by hepatic LDL-receptor pathways and affect apoB particle residence time. Genetic backgrounds involving LDLR, APOB, PCSK9, or other lipid-metabolic loci may influence baseline LDL and response, but ordinary consumer genotypes are **not validated keto-response predictors**. A severe keto-associated LDL rise can resemble familial hypercholesterolemia; diet-associated reversibility and inherited FH should not be treated as mutually exclusive. [S04, S08, S09, S20]

### Discordance: LDL cargo versus apoB count

If LDL particles are more cholesterol-rich, LDL-C may increase disproportionately relative to apoB/LDL-P. If particle number rises, apoB rises as well. The response is heterogeneous: some longer interventions report higher LDL-C with stable *average* apoB; short randomized high-fat feeding studies report increases in **both** LDL-C and apoB. Neither low TG nor large LDL diameter establishes an absence of atherogenic particles. [S04, S06, S07, S18, S19]

## 3. Direct randomized evidence without substantial weight-loss dependence

| Source | Study design | LDL, apoB and weight-specific findings | Proper inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[S26] Retterstøl 2018** | Randomized parallel, 3 weeks; 15 LCHF (<20 g carbs/d) and 15 habitual-diet controls completing, normal-weight young adults | LDL-C 2.2→3.1 mmol/L in LCHF; **44%** higher LDL change vs controls; apoB significantly increased. No statistically significant differences in **body weight or body composition**, nor TG. Individual LDL change 5–107%. | A marked LDL/apoB response does not require demonstrable fat loss in a short trial; study is small, >2-fold saturated-fat exposure compared with controls and not an 8-week maintenance experiment |
| **[S04] Burén 2021** | Randomized crossover feeding; 17 completers; 4-week ketogenic LCHF versus control, intended stable weight | LDL-C +1.82 mmol/L (95% CI 1.24–2.39); apoB and both large/small LDL fractions increased; effect not explained by minor weight change alone | Experimental support for direct dietary influence; duration short, women only; cannot isolate ketosis from specific fat composition |
| **[S05] Keto-Med 2022** | Randomized crossover; 33 complete cases; 12-week ketogenic vs Mediterranean-plus in T2D/prediabetes | LDL-C +10% keto vs −5% Mediterranean; TG −16% vs −5%, no significant primary HbA1c difference | Illustrates biomarker tradeoffs, **not** a pure maintenance or LMHR study; use authors' corrected figure axis |
| **[S07] CARBFUNC 2026** | Randomized low- vs higher-carb in initial obesity, 193 baseline; 2-year trial | LCHF LDL-C and larger LDL subfractions rose, while within-arm apoB increase was not significant; TG-rich lipoproteins decreased | Long exposure but **84% attrition** at 2 years and concurrent weight changes; cannot interpret as a true weight-stabilized trial |
| **[S27] Carter 2025** | Three-way isocaloric randomized crossover, 5 weeks per diet, 48 completed all phases; 6% vs 12% energy from SFA, with differing egg/cholesterol intake | Lower saturated-fat phase had modestly lower LDL-C than high-saturated-fat reference; effect of SFA on LDL was stronger than dietary cholesterol in model; LDL size distribution also changed | Direct dietary-fat composition test, **not ketogenic**. Published corrigendum exists: verify corrected specifics before using exact numerical endpoints |

These are complementary but do not form a factorial trial separating carbohydrate restriction, saturated fat, body recomposition and genetic background. S26 is especially valuable because it **measured body composition** and did not observe significant between-group change, although absence of statistical significance in a 30-person study is not proof of identical body composition in every participant.

## 4. Fat source matters; low carbohydrate is not a single dietary exposure

A person eating 20 g carbohydrate/day predominantly from butter, cheese, cream and coconut oil differs materially from a person eating the same carbohydrate amount with olive oil, fish, nuts, avocado and an adequate supply of low-carbohydrate fibrous plants. Saturated fatty acids have different individual effects depending on chain length and the food matrix, and isocaloric replacement with mono- or polyunsaturated fatty acids generally lowers LDL-C in controlled feeding literature. Dietary cholesterol also varies in effect. [S20, S27]

Three design distinctions matter:

1. **Within-keto fat substitution**: maintain similar carbohydrate grams, calories, protein, fiber, dietary cholesterol and weight while swapping saturated for unsaturated fats. This is the cleanest dietary test of fat composition during ketosis.
2. **Carbohydrate reintroduction**: a drop in LDL-C when adding carbohydrate can reflect ketosis status, energy intake, fat replacement and/or altered fat composition. In small case series LDL-C and apoB fell without medication, but the intervention does **not** identify a unique causal pathway. [S08, S09]
3. **Weight stabilization**: the diet can change LDL-C without major weight loss (S04, S26), and stabilized scale weight in a case report does not prove constant adipose mass (S08). True repeated DXA/MRI confirmation is not present in the long case histories.

**Saturated-fat meta-regression caution:** S10 did not find a statistically significant *study-level* association between saturated-fat intake and LDL change. This does not refute the ability of a randomized isocaloric SFA-vs-unsaturated-fat contrast to alter LDL, because between-study confounding, limited reporting and exposure ranges can obscure within-person effects.

### Proposed clinically supervised n-of-1 design for reversible biomarkers

To test how food composition affects one individual's LDL-C and apoB—not to diagnose mechanism or predict MI risk—record baseline diet/medication and weight trend, alter one dietary variable at a time, document adherence and macros, repeat the same lipid laboratory/apoB after a defined 4–12 week interval under consistent conditions. Crossovers can improve internal validity but still have carryover and time effects. Major LDL-C/apoB elevations warrant clinician assessment without delaying guideline-directed prevention solely to finish an unproven plateau experiment. [S20]

### Retrospective severe LDL-C chart series [S33]

A 2023 referral-based case series identified **17** ketogenic-diet users with severe LDL-C elevations (mean on-diet LDL-C **316 mg/dL**). Among **13** with follow-up, mean LDL-C was approximately **143 mg/dL** after dietary change/discontinuation, often with lipid-lowering treatment; **8** had started statins. ApoB, available in only **10**, averaged **191 mg/dL**, showing that at least some severe ketogenic LDL-C rises are accompanied by substantial atherogenic particle elevation rather than mere cholesterol enrichment. The study does not isolate diet from treatment or quantify cardiovascular risk. [S33]

## 5. Imaging evidence and the retraction that materially changes interpretation

### Baseline KETO-CTA study, 2024 — [S13]

An observational cross-sectional analysis compared **80** carbohydrate-restricted participants with marked diet-associated LDL elevation and **80** matched comparators. Mean LDL-C was about 272 versus 123 mg/dL; mean ketogenic exposure around 4.7 years. No statistically significant difference in baseline plaque burden was detected, and LDL-C was not significantly correlated with plaque *within that selected cohort*.

This does **not** demonstrate equal lifetime event risk, an equivalence bound, or safety of very high apoB. An absence of statistically significant difference is compatible with underpowering, short exposure relative to atherosclerosis natural history, selection, residual confounding, young age and differences before ketogenic initiation. The study's cross-sectional association cannot directly measure plaque progression.

### 2025 longitudinal KETO-CTA paper — **RETRACTED** [S14]

The later 2025 article titled *Longitudinal Data From the KETO-CTA Study: Plaque Predicts Plaque, ApoB Does Not* was retracted in 2026 because methodological problems affected the data's reliability. **Do not use its claims, figures or analyses as valid evidence that apoB is unrelated to plaque progression or that keto-associated LDL is safe.** The 2024 baseline study is a *different* article and must be described separately, with its own limited design.

### CCTA or CAC negative in an individual

A zero coronary calcium score or no detectable plaque at one time is helpful clinical context in appropriately selected patients, not a biological experiment showing that high apoB particles cannot cause disease. A younger patient may have little calcification despite substantial lifetime future exposure; CCTA sensitivity and risk horizons are not unlimited. Observational case histories cannot measure counterfactual risk.

## 6. What evidence would persuade in either direction?

| Hypothesis | Strong supporting experiment | What would undermine it |
|---|---|---|
| LMHR lipid rise is driven by VLDL energy trafficking | Isocaloric randomized carbohydrate crossover with tracer-confirmed VLDL-TG production and VLDL-to-LDL apoB conversion, replicated in lean and nonlean groups | No rise in VLDL flux/conversion despite pronounced LDL rise, or clearance changes explaining most of response |
| Persistent elevated LDL after weight plateau is residual adipose cholesterol release | Repeated measured fat-mass loss with cholesterol tracer evidence and fall in LDL upon cessation of *fat loss*, unchanged diet | Stable fat mass plus continuing high LDL; large LDL reductions solely on matched-weight SFA swap |
| LDL-C elevation is cargo-only in some patients | Elevated LDL-C with relatively low/unchanged apoB and LDL-P, repeatedly on matched lab methods | ApoB and total LDL-P also rise markedly |
| High apoB in LMHR has no effect on atherosclerosis | Adequately powered longitudinal events at sustained high and low apoB with reliable exposure and extensive adjustment, or experimentally causal contrasts | Plaque/events track cumulative apoB exposure after accounting for selection and confounding |

The last claim is particularly hard to test with small CCTA cohorts because absence of coronary events in 1–5 years does not adequately measure differences in lifetime exposure. General genetic, pathophysiological and intervention evidence for apoB-containing particles remains relevant pending phenotype-specific outcomes.

## 7. Clinical and communication safeguards

- Do not present LMHR as a diagnosis requiring diet-specific rules or as “immune” to atherosclerosis.
- Do not presume familial hypercholesterolemia if LDL rises on keto, but do not exclude it merely because LDL falls with dietary modification.
- Do not equate LDL-C with apoB in every patient; check both when results are unusual.
- Do not claim TG/HDL improvement outweighs high particle number without evidence.
- The 2026 guideline treats LDL-C **≥190 mg/dL and/or apoB ≥140 mg/dL** as severe-hypercholesterolemia context warranting evaluation and clinician-directed treatment consideration. [S20]
- A source retraction is an explicit negative evidence-status event, not merely “controversy.” [S14]
- Do not use cross-ancestry extrapolation to assert that Filipino LMHR profiles are safe; see [03_SOUTHEAST_ASIA](03_SOUTHEAST_ASIA.md).

## Bottom line

Short randomized studies directly show LDL-C and apoB can rise on very-low-carbohydrate, high-fat diets **without demonstrable weight loss**, supporting ongoing dietary and lipoprotein-metabolic effects instead of attributing all elevations to adipose cholesterol released during active weight reduction. Some other long interventions show predominantly LDL cholesterol enrichment without a significant mean apoB increase. Neither response establishes long-term safety of sustained high LDL-C/apoB. LMHR is a useful phenotype hypothesis, but its mechanisms and reliable long-term clinical event risks remain unresolved.

## Executive conclusions — evidence graded

| Claim | Current judgment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Lipolysis occurs even without net weight loss | Established physiology | Mechanistic context; not by itself an LDL-C pathway |
| Large active weight loss can be accompanied by transient increases in serum cholesterol | Demonstrated in a **small** longitudinal human series | [S01]; counterpoint [S02] |
| Cholesterol released by shrinking adipose tissue **causes** sustained high LDL-C during ≥8 weeks of stable weight/recomposition | **Not demonstrated**; no validated lag time | [S01], [S02], [S03], [S21] |
| Ketogenic/high-fat feeding can markedly raise LDL-C and apoB with an attempt to keep weight stable | Direct experimental support in a selected formulation/population | [S04] |
| Low TG, higher HDL and high LDL-C can coexist | Well described; not equivalent to low particle number | [S05], [S06], [S07], [S10] |
| ApoB or NMR LDL-P may clarify cholesterol-mass versus particle-number discordance | Supported by outcomes cohorts and consensus | [S18], [S19], [S20] |
| Large LDL size or high HDL neutralizes elevated apoB/LDL exposure | **Not established** | [S18], [S19], [S20] |
| Lower BMI predicts stronger average LDL rise on low-carbohydrate trials | Trial meta-analysis association; mechanism unresolved | [S10] |
| The lipid energy model is experimentally proven | **No**; mechanism/human kinetic predictions remain to be tested | [S12] |
| Longitudinal KETO-CTA results show apoB does not drive plaque | **Invalid claim: the paper was retracted** | [S14] |
| Filipinos have a proven uniquely safe LDL-C/apoB profile on keto | **No data establishing this** | [S15], [S16], [S17] |

### Nuances lost in a simple “cholesterol from fat loss” explanation

1. Adipocyte triglyceride hydrolysis, cellular cholesterol trafficking, hepatic VLDL production, LDL particle remodeling and LDL clearance are **distinct compartments**. Continuous lipolysis does not show that adipocyte cholesterol is being exported into LDL. [S01, S02, S12]
2. Stable body **weight** can conceal concurrent fat loss and lean-mass gain; however, true recomposition requires repeated objective body-composition data, and even measured fat loss does not identify cholesterol flux. [S21]
3. In the original six-person weight-loss series, total cholesterol **decreased after two-plus months of maintenance**, not increased indefinitely. Adipose cholesterol concentration increased in abdominal samples and did not change at peripheral sampled sites. [S01]
4. A randomized ketogenic feeding trial raised **apoB as well as LDL-C** in normal-weight women without needing prolonged large weight loss. Neither “large LDL only” nor “adipose release only” adequately describes all cases. [S04]
5. A 2026 two-year trial in participants with obesity did **not** find a significant within-arm apoB increase despite greater large-LDL cholesterol on a highly saturated-fat low-carbohydrate diet. This contrasts with the short feeding trial; generalization between samples is limited. [S07]
6. The 2026 Southeast Asian cohort has a **source-internal ethnicity sample-count inconsistency** (paper text versus table headers), flagged for resolution. Reported lipoprotein concentrations remain descriptive, not ancestry-specific ketogenic responses. [S16]

## Practical interpretive hierarchy (for clinician-led discussion, not an app rule)

- Confirm exact prior and current lipid measurements (assay and LDL calculation), lipid-lowering medications, dietary carbohydrate grams, saturation/unsaturation and cholesterol sources, fiber, recent energy deficit, weight and fat-mass trajectories.
- Measure **apoB** when LDL-C seems discordant with TG/HDL or clinical risk; measure **Lp(a)** at least once as guideline-recommended risk context. Non-HDL-C is a useful ordinary lipid-panel value, but often largely mirrors LDL-C when TG is low. [S19, S20]
- NMR LDL-P can provide particle-number characterization if the apoB result is unavailable, unexpectedly discordant, or a subclass question genuinely changes management. LDL particle **size** does not cancel high apoB. [S18, S19, S23]
- Consider alternate explanations (e.g., thyroid disease, nephrotic disease, medication, familial hypercholesterolemia) and severe LDL/apoB clinical guidance. The 2026 guideline identifies LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL and/or apoB ≥140 mg/dL as severe-hypercholesterolemia context. [S20]
- An organized dietary perturbation can clarify **reversibility** of biomarkers: reduce saturated fat in favor of unsaturated fat while holding other variables as constant as feasible, or review carbohydrate intake with a clinician, then repeat lipids/apoB. It does not identify a unique mechanism or measure hard cardiovascular outcomes. [S04, S08, S09]
- Coronary calcium or CCTA in selected patients may refine current plaque assessment; a negative scan does not establish the lifelong safety of severe apoB exposure. [S13, S14, S20]

## Explicit nonclaims

- No “eight-week washout rule” is known for adipose cholesterol release.
- Do not claim lower TG or higher HDL **proves** elevated LDL-C is safe.
- Do not infer Filipino or Southeast Asian genotype-to-fat recommendations from a cross-sectional interaction.
- Do not substitute observational CAC/CCTA for randomized long-term clinical endpoints.
- Do not invoke the **retracted** KETO-CTA longitudinal analysis as reassuring evidence.
- Do not automatically integrate findings into ideal-weight calculations, public lab suggestions, ancestry defaults, or medication advice.

## Study-type hierarchy

**Highest available for short-term dietary biomarker causation:** controlled feeding and randomized dietary interventions [S04, S05, S07]. **For longer cardiovascular risk:** observational event cohorts, apoB biology, clinical guideline synthesis [S18, S19, S20]; currently no ketogenic LMHR outcomes randomized trial. **For adipose mobilization:** tiny longitudinal sampling studies [S01, S02] plus research gaps. **For specific diet reversibility:** individual case series [S08, S09], not treatment-effect estimates. **For ancestry:** observational risk and biomarker distributions [S15–S17], not direct response trials.

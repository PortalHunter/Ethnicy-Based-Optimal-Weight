# 03 — Filipino and broader Southeast Asian evidence on LDL, apoB, particles and diet

**Bottom line:** There is evidence that Filipinos and other Southeast Asian groups differ in conventional lipid distributions and cardiometabolic risk, and there are limited studies of apoB. **No robust evidence presently demonstrates an ancestry-specific ketogenic LDL-C/apoB response or a different safety interpretation of high apoB in Filipino people.** Do not merge Filipino, Malay, Vietnamese, Chinese, Indian, and generic “Asian” groups into one risk category.

## 1. Scope and causality boundaries

Possible sources of between-group differences include allele frequency, adipose distribution, baseline metabolic health, traditional and acculturated diets, access to prevention, medications, age/sex composition, immigration selection, economic conditions and study recruitment. A cross-sectional ethnic comparison is **not** proof of inherited susceptibility, and high/low group means cannot predict the response of one individual.

**South Asian ancestry** (e.g., parts of the Indian subcontinent) has a different clinical risk-discussion status from **Southeast Asian** Filipino ancestry. Do not import South Asian guideline risk-enhancer language wholesale into Filipino interpretation. The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline also discusses cardiometabolic risk among Filipino Americans, but does not validate Filipino-specific apoB safety cutoffs. [S20]

## 2. Direct Filipino saturated-fat study: FiLWHEL, 2023 — [S15]

- Population: **406** Filipino women who migrated to Korea through marriage; dietary exposure collected by 24-hour recall. No low-carbohydrate randomization, no fasting-ketone confirmation, no matched non-Filipino intervention group.
- Design: cross-sectional multivariable model; investigators modeled substitution of carbohydrate with saturated fat.
- Highest versus lowest tertile association: high LDL-C (≥130 mg/dL) OR **4.00** (95% CI **1.48–10.79**), high total cholesterol OR **3.62** (1.53–8.55), with trend P=0.02 for high LDL-C.
- Small genetic finding: rs6102059 near MAFB appeared to modify the **composite dyslipidemia** association (P-interaction=0.01), but **not the LDL-C-specific association** (P-interaction=0.28). Three other candidate SNP interaction tests were not significant.
- Meaning: gives a legitimate Filipino-specific observational anchor consistent with higher saturated fat and LDL-C; **does not** prove a Filipino-specific ketogenic reaction, a causal genotype-by-diet interaction, or a personalized fat allowance.

Confounding by total diet quality, recall error, social variables and multiple testing need consideration. “Substitution” in an observational model is not equivalent to a controlled randomized isocaloric swap. Primary full text: [FiLWHEL](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10275172/).

## 3. Filipino and neighboring populations: apoB and Lp(a)

### Singapore LPACO cohort (2026) — [S16]

A predominantly female healthcare worker cohort, 2024 recruitment, analyzed apoB, directly measured LDL-C, non-HDL-C and Lp(a) in a 2026 publication. Useful descriptive concentrations cited in the authors’ prose:

| Group | Median apoB (mg/dL) | Mean LDL-C (mmol/L) | Mean non-HDL-C (mmol/L) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Filipino | 90 | 3.11 | 3.53 |
| Malay | 92 | 3.21 | 3.61 |
| Chinese | 87 | 3.08 | 3.45 |
| Indian | 95 | 3.18 | 3.64 |

**Source audit warning — subgroup denominator discrepancy:** The paper's narrative says n=1,927, ethnic percentages 57.2% Chinese / 16.9% Malay / 9.2% Indian / 12.8% Filipino / 3.8% other (implying about 325 Malay and 247 Filipino). A rendered table representation displays a total n=1,928 and headers suggesting n=247 Malay / n=325 Filipino. Those two statements conflict; **do not report a precise Filipino n as resolved** until an official correction or author clarification is obtained. See [S16] and [continuation](CONTINUE.md). The directly reported concentration data are shown as published; their group assignment should be independently checked against the table before secondary statistical analyses.

Overall cohort median apoB was 89 mg/dL, with 49.2% at apoB ≥90 and 10.8% at ≥120 mg/dL. Lp(a) was a distinct contributor to total apoB; the Lp(a)-to-apoB particle proportion exceeded 5% in about 11.4% of participants. The paper reports a **median**, not maximum, proportion ~14.9% for people with Lp(a) ≥250 nmol/L.

**Limitations:** observational healthcare-worker selection (not population-representative), many women, one-time blood tests, some statin use, nonfasting sampling, limited clinical events. **Not** a study of ketogenic diets, LDL-P by NMR, or prospective Filipino ASCVD risk at matched apoB.

### Philippine LIPAS pilot (2018) — NEW primary Filipino apoB evidence

**[Apolipoprotein Levels in Patients with Acute Coronary Syndrome (LIPAS), Philippine Journal of Internal Medicine](https://pjim.pcp.org.ph/elib/journal/identifier/2017-054/pdf).**

- **95** adult ACS patients at Philippine General Hospital sampled within 24 hours after admission in 2015–2016; 64% male, mean age ~59 years, about **31–32%** taking statins before admission.
- Mean LDL-C ~**109 mg/dL**, non-HDL-C ~**135 mg/dL**, HDL-C ~**36.9 mg/dL**; mean apoB ~**103.8 mg/dL**. Under the paper's specified apoB threshold <80 mg/dL, **82%** were classified as elevated. The study reports high Lp(a) by its own cutoff in 42%.
- Mean LDL-C among statin-treated participants ~85 mg/dL while mean apoB ~87.6 mg/dL.
- **Interpretation:** confirms that apoB was clinically measurable and often elevated in a small Filipino ACS sample even with LDL-C that was not extreme.
- **Strong cautions:** selection only of people **already having ACS** (no matched healthy controls), acute-phase measurement, a substantial proportion on statins, historical reference cutoffs differing from current clinical guidance; no demonstration that apoB predicts *future* events in Filipinos, no low-TG/high-HDL phenotype and no ketogenic exposure. Do not extrapolate the 82% prevalence to the Filipino general population.

This primary Philippine journal article adds local apoB evidence missing from the initial conversation review; it should be compared with larger prospective Filipino cohorts when available.

### Vietnamese apoB/LDL discordance study (October 2026) — [S17]

Doan et al. recruited **106** ACS cases and **70** non-ACS controls in Vietnam. ApoB averaged ~103.3 versus 78.1 mg/dL; the study reported 22% apoB/LDL-C discordance and higher cross-validated ACS discrimination with apoB than LDL-C (AUC 0.665 versus 0.478).

**Limitations:** this is a *diagnostic case-control comparison of a syndrome already present*, not a prospective prevention cohort. Acute illness, baseline medications and selection can make direct LDL-C comparisons atypical. It supports studying apoB in Southeast Asia, not a validated ketogenic-specific test or LDL-C risk exemption.

## 4. Cardiometabolic context without over-interpreting ancestry

The 2004 study comparing **181 Filipino** and **196 white** older women found more diabetes, metabolic syndrome, higher TG and greater visceral fat among Filipinas, **but not more measured moderate/extensive coronary calcium** at the observed cross-sectional time point. This is a useful reminder that population-level risk-factor differences do not automatically translate into a uniform coronary calcium outcome at every age. The study cannot test future event risk at fixed apoB or ketogenic diet effects. [Subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, PMID 15505100](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15505100/).

A review of Filipino American cardiometabolic health documents underrepresentation and heterogeneity in diabetes, hypertension and lipid data and cautions against reducing community differences to single genes. [Filipino American cardiometabolic disparities, PMID 34837163](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34837163/). This review is useful context but is not a trial or ancestry-specific LDL risk estimator.

## 5. NMR particle size distributions: what the literature does *not* establish

Targeted searches of Filipino LDL particle size, NMR LDL-P, apoB, low-carbohydrate, and neighboring Southeast Asian studies found limited directly matched data. The sources above include conventional LDL-C, apoB, Lp(a) and at times lipidomic species, **not validated Filipino ketogenic NMR subfraction distributions**.

Clinical NMR LDL-P estimates concentration of lipoprotein particles, whereas **lipidomics** assays chemical lipid molecules and cannot be treated as an LDL-P surrogate. Even if a Filipino group average had more large LDL particles, that would not demonstrate a safe high-apoB threshold.

Importantly, MESA's four broad US race/ethnicity groups included **Chinese Americans**, but a Chinese American NMR risk estimate should not be relabeled “Filipino” or “Southeast Asian.” [S18]

## 6. Research questions to pursue next

1. **Matched feeding:** randomize Filipino, Malay, Vietnamese and comparator ancestry groups to identical low-carbohydrate diets, prospectively varying saturated versus unsaturated fat while holding calories, protein and fiber constant; measure LDL-C, apoB, non-HDL, TG, HDL, Lp(a), LDL-P, direct LDL-C and weight/fat mass before and after 4, 8, 12, 24 weeks.
2. **Within-group replication:** validate FiLWHEL dietary-fat signal in women *and men*, across migration contexts, with repeated exposure and cholesterol-absorption/clearance biomarkers. Prespecify rs6102059 as exploratory only unless independently replicated for the LDL-specific endpoint.
3. **Prospective risk:** estimate Filipino-specific cardiovascular event rates and subclinical plaque development across apoB strata with adjustment for age, treatments, diabetes, education, smoking, environment, statin use and lifetime exposure.
4. **Assay access and standards:** characterize local apoB calibration and Lp(a) molar methods, distinguishing direct and calculated LDL-C; record whether NMR LDL-P reference ranges are validated for Asian participants.
5. **Denominator audit:** resolve the Singapore 1,927/1,928 total and Malay/Filipino subgroup count mismatch from publication or supplemental material before making subgroup-powered statements.

## Conclusions

Filipino-specific observational evidence suggests a clinically relevant relationship between saturated-fat intake and high LDL-C, and limited Filipino clinical datasets show apoB can add information. What remains **unproven** is that being Filipino causes a unique ketogenic LDL response, that large LDL is harmless in Filipinos, or that standard severe LDL-C/apoB guidance should be disregarded by ancestry.

# Research Team 3 — Pharmacogenetics Research Review

**Project:** Ancestry-Adjusted Optimal Body Weight & Nutrition Calculator  
**Evidence cut-off:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** Research synthesis; **not** an implementation specification or medical advice  
**Repository reviewed:** https://github.com/PortalHunter/Ethnicy-Based-Optimal-Weight  
**Calculator reviewed:** https://github.com/PortalHunter/Ethnicy-Based-Optimal-Weight/blob/main/optimal_body_weight_calculator-9.html

## Executive conclusion

A **separate, linked educational pharmacogenetics module** is defensible. Pharmacogenetics (PGx) can explain how *confirmed* variants affect particular medicines, why population allele frequencies differ, and when genetic-testing questions may be appropriate for a clinician. It does **not** provide a scientifically defensible means of adjusting BMI thresholds, defining an individual's ideal weight, predicting their response to a drug from ancestry, or issuing individual treatment recommendations. No medication choice or dosage should be generated by the existing calculator.

**Three decisions follow from the evidence:**
1. Publish independently verified, gene–drug *reference entries* and distinguish CPIC/DPWG guidance, FDA label statements, and actual evidence of improved clinical outcomes.
2. Present ancestry-associated allele frequencies only in a separate population-context section with denominator, sampling, source and limitations; **never** calculate an individual's predicted genotype from self-identification or ancestry percentages.
3. Treat GLP-1/GIP weight-treatment genomics as **research**, not present-day patient-specific PGx. A 2026 association was independently replicated, but no validated clinical decision rule or treatment-utility evidence follows from that association.

## 1. Scientific foundations

**Pharmacogenetics** studies effects of individual genetic differences on drug response. **Pharmacogenomics** usually refers to broader genomic variation or systematic studies of many genes; the terms overlap. Drug response reflects genotype **plus** age, kidney/liver function, indication, co-medication, dose and exposure, adherence, disease, nutrition, organ function and environmental factors. This is distinct from pharmacogenetic *prediction* of a clinical outcome.

### Mechanisms and evidence chain

| Mechanism | Examples | Clinically relevant distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Absorption/distribution/metabolism/excretion (PK)** | CYP2C19 activates clopidogrel; CYP2D6 activates codeine; CYP2C9 affects warfarin; CYP3A5 metabolizes tacrolimus | Altered drug/metabolite exposure may not translate into proven clinical benefit from testing; some drugs are prodrugs, so faster metabolism can increase rather than decrease active metabolite. |
| **Transport** | SLCO1B1/OATP1B1 hepatic statin uptake; ABCG2 efflux | A pharmacokinetic signal can precede or exceed certainty about muscle injury risk. |
| **Pharmacodynamic targets** | VKORC1 determines warfarin sensitivity; GLP1R and GIPR receptor variants under investigation | Target-gene association alone is not a genotype-based prescribing rule. |
| **Immune susceptibility** | HLA-B*15:02–carbamazepine; HLA-A*31:01–carbamazepine; HLA-B*58:01–allopurinol; HLA-B*57:01–abacavir | Presence raises *risk* of a rare potentially severe immune reaction; absence never guarantees safety. |
| **Detoxification and organ toxicity** | TPMT/NUDT15–thiopurines; DPYD–fluoropyrimidines; MT-RNR1–aminoglycosides | Rare or untested variants, pharmacologic exposure and non-genetic contributors remain important. |

**Critical separation:** (a) analytical validity (does a laboratory accurately detect the allele?), (b) clinical validity (does it relate to a relevant drug outcome?), (c) clinical utility (does using the result improve a patient outcome?), and (d) health-system feasibility. Neither allele prevalence nor a small association study establishes all four.

### Evidence categories for this project

- **Guideline-supported:** A specific clinical gene–drug recommendation is provided by a current professional guideline (e.g., CPIC) or a clinically actionable regulator-supported association. Attach exact phenotype, indication, strength and version. This *does not* mean every person should be tested.
- **Observational:** Reproducible association or population difference without evidence sufficient to make the particular proposed clinical action routine.
- **Exploratory:** Early GWAS/candidate-gene/pharmacodynamic findings requiring stronger replication or utility trials.
- **Insufficient:** The intended clinical conclusion cannot be drawn (including absence of an endorsed recommendation).
- **Contradicted:** Meaningful negative or inconsistent evidence conflicts with a proposed deterministic claim. This label should apply to the *specific claim*, not necessarily to the gene–drug field generally.

CPIC's recommendations answer **how to interpret a result if available**, not **whether to order a test**. The FDA pharmacogenetic-associations table distinguishes therapeutic-management recommendations, associations with potential effects on safety or response, and pharmacokinetic-only associations; *inclusion is not blanket FDA endorsement of testing*. FDA labels and CPIC/DPWG can use different evidentiary scopes. ClinPGx, which replaced the PharmGKB website in **July 2025**, curates associations and guideline annotations, while PharmVar standardizes allele/haplotype nomenclature. A ClinPGx association level is **not identical** to CPIC strength or the FDA table category. [S1–S6]

## 2. Gene–drug dossiers: actionable examples and bounds

### 2.1 CYP2C19 and clopidogrel

- **Relevant alleles:** CYP2C19 *2 (rs4244285), *3 (rs4986893) loss of function; *17 (rs12248560) increased function. Other loss-of-function alleles and phased diplotypes also matter. The *2/*17 combination should not be mistaken for uniformly rapid metabolism.
- **Drug/response:** Clopidogrel is a prodrug; intermediate/poor metabolizers produce less active metabolite and have reduced platelet inhibition, with increased ischemic-event risk in relevant settings.
- **Professional guidance:** CPIC 2022 supports alternative P2Y12-inhibitor consideration for intermediate/poor metabolizers in **ACS/PCI**, with differing certainty outside these indications and in neurovascular disease. This is a guideline summary, **not an instruction for a patient to switch therapy**.
- **Evidence of utility/uncertainty:** TAILOR-PCI (n=5,302; primary analysis 1,849 loss-of-function carriers) observed 12-month composite ischemic outcomes in 4.0% vs 5.9%; HR **0.66 (95% CI 0.43–1.02), p=0.06**, **not statistically significant** for its prespecified primary endpoint. That result does not erase pharmacologic and aggregate evidence, but rules out claiming that this particular trial definitively proved superiority. [S7,S18]
- **Limitations:** Stenting/indication, comorbidity, interacting medicines, genotype coverage, local prescribing contraindications and adherence. Allele frequency does not reveal an individual's genotype.

### 2.2 HLA-B*15:02 and HLA-A*31:01 with carbamazepine

- **Specific alleles:** HLA-B*15:02 (distinct from other HLA-B*15 alleles) strongly predicts carbamazepine-associated **Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN)** in studied populations; HLA-A*31:01 is linked to a wider hypersensitivity phenotype including maculopapular eruption and DRESS, and sometimes SJS/TEN.
- **Clinical guidance:** CPIC 2017/2018 provides genotype-specific guidance, especially for treatment-naïve individuals. FDA labeling emphasizes HLA-B*15:02 screening consideration in ancestry groups where the allele occurs, and warns that even a negative test does not eliminate risk.
- **Population:** Label-reported HLA-B*15:02 *carrier* estimates exceed 15% in some Hong Kong/Thai/Malaysian/Philippine samples, about 10% in Taiwan, around 4% in North China, 2–4% among broad South Asian samples, and <1% in Japan/Korea. These are heterogeneous label summaries, **not transferrable individual probabilities**. HLA-A*31:01 geography is different. [S8,S19]
- **Limitations:** HLA typing resolution, positive predictive value for a rare event, prior tolerance, co-medications and clinical vigilance.

### 2.3 HLA-B*58:01 and allopurinol

- **Outcome:** Severe cutaneous adverse reactions including SJS/TEN/DRESS. A 2025 living meta-analysis of 24 case-control studies (13,719 participants) found OR **117.6 (95% CI 70.3–196.8)** for severe skin reactions among carriers; observational case-control OR is **not** absolute individual risk. [S20]
- **Clinical guidance:** CPIC 2015/2016 provides an allele-specific alternative-treatment recommendation for carriers. ACR 2020 **conditionally** recommends *pre-treatment testing* for people of Southeast Asian descent and African American patients, while conditionally recommending against blanket testing elsewhere; these are distinct statements from CPIC's interpretation of a known result. [S9,S10]
- **Limits:** Risk persists in noncarriers; kidney dysfunction and other factors change risk. Prevalence is variable even within named populations. Recent U.S. allopurinol labels provide examples including ~8–10% Han Chinese, ~8% Thai, ~6% Korean, ~4% Black, and <1% European/Japanese frequencies, but labels and studies may use nonidentical populations and metrics. [S21]

### 2.4 TPMT and NUDT15 with thiopurines

- **Drugs:** Azathioprine, mercaptopurine, thioguanine.
- **Functional alleles:** TPMT *2, *3A, *3C among relevant decreased/no-function alleles; NUDT15 c.415C>T (p.Arg139Cys, now within *3 suballeles), and other decreased/no-function alleles. **One gene is not a substitute for the other.**
- **Outcome:** Impaired thiopurine processing greatly raises risk of severe myelosuppression. The **2025 CPIC update (published 2026)** adds distinct categories for **compound TPMT/NUDT15 intermediate metabolism**, includes malignancy vs nonmalignancy considerations and revises allele-function assignments. It specifically retired the former independent NUDT15*2 star designation in favor of *3 suballeles. **The June 2026 formal correction** additionally fixed a TPMT phenotype table error: one no-function + one decreased-function allele is **intermediate**, not possible intermediate. Use the corrected table and current online allele definitions. [S11,S32]
- **Population:** NUDT15 dysfunction is relatively important in many East Asian and Indigenous American/Latino-derived samples; TPMT alleles occur widely, with different spectra. **Never** infer either from ethnicity.
- **Limits:** TPMT enzyme phenotyping can be influenced by recent transfusion; incomplete panels and novel alleles miss risk; CBC and liver tests remain essential regardless of genotype. Do not translate historical tables into a current rule engine.

### 2.5 CYP2D6 and selected medications

- **Gene complexities:** CYP2D6 *4/*5 often no-function, *10/*17/*29 diminished function; structural deletions, duplications, hybrid alleles and inhibitors can cause **phenoconversion**. CYP2D6*5 represents deletion; duplication of functional alleles can increase activity. Panel content matters as much as reporting a phenotype.
- **Codeine/tramadol (CPIC 2021):** Poor metabolizers form less active metabolite and may lack analgesia; ultrarapid metabolizers can form excessive active metabolite and have serious toxicity. Both phenotypes have clinically actionable guidance; **oxycodone, methadone and OPRM1/COMT** do not receive equivalent opioid prescribing rules from that guideline. [S14]
- **Atomoxetine (CPIC 2019):** Pharmacokinetic exposure in CYP2D6 poor metabolizers averaged approximately **10-fold** that in non-poor metabolizers in the guideline's source summary; it is not a 10-fold change in clinical efficacy or a suggested dose factor. [S22]
- **Metoprolol (CPIC 2024):** Poor-metabolizer guidance reflects higher exposure and bradycardia risk; clinical outcome effects remain uncertain. Other beta blockers and ADRB1/ADRB2/ADRA2C/GRK4/GRK5 did not meet its clinical recommendation threshold. [S16]
- **Antidepressants (CPIC 2023):** Some CYP2D6/CYP2C19/CYP2B6–drug pairs are actionable (e.g., paroxetine/CYP2D6, escitalopram/CYP2C19, sertraline/CYP2C19 or CYP2B6); **SLC6A4 and HTR2A** tests have inadequate evidence for clinical selection. A blanket antidepressant-response prediction based on a proprietary composite test is not supported by the CPIC guideline. [S15]

### 2.6 SLCO1B1/ABCG2/CYP2C9 and statins

- **Gene/alleles:** SLCO1B1 reduced function is often tagged by c.521T>C (rs4149056), carried on multiple star-allele haplotypes. ABCG2 c.421C>A (rs2231142) matters for rosuvastatin; CYP2C9 *2/*3 and other alleles matter for fluvastatin.
- **Outcome:** Statin exposure and probability of statin-associated musculoskeletal symptoms, with drug- and gene-specific evidence; not an automatic prediction of myopathy, rhabdomyolysis or lipid-lowering effectiveness.
- **CPIC:** 2022 recommendations span simvastatin and several other statins; **September 2025 online SLCO1B1 curation** changed function assignments for alleles, notably in underrepresented populations. FDA Table Section 3 still lists some SLCO1B1 statin entries as **pharmacokinetic-only**. The difference reflects the scope of two resources, not permission to infer a patient's risk from ancestry. [S12,S2]

### 2.7 DPYD and fluoropyrimidines

- **Examples:** DPYD *2A (c.1905+1G>A), *13 (c.1679T>G), c.2846A>T, and intronic c.1129-5923C>G/HapB3 (rs75017182); more numerous variants are relevant and many standard panels do not capture all deficient individuals.
- **Outcome:** Decreased dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase activity and potentially life-threatening toxicity from 5-fluorouracil/capecitabine. This is a well-supported genotype-directed safety area.
- **Critical live-version issue:** A **July 9, 2026** CPIC interim note indicates a future revision for *the specific causal HapB3 variant*, while explicitly stating the change would **not** appear in the main database until guideline publication. As checked for this review, the ClinPGx page continued to mark the comprehensive revision **in development**. The module must mark the pending change as **not finalized** and never calculate clinician doses. [S13]
- **Equity:** A review of non-European populations found that c.557A>G (p.Tyr186Cys) in some people with African ancestry is often missing from four-variant European-centric panels; functional data and small clinical series support concern, but panel validity and clinical implementation need separate local validation. [S23]

### 2.8 Other strong examples

- **HLA-B*57:01–abacavir:** Avoidance in confirmed carriers is strongly supported. In PREDICT-1, prospective screening reduced immunologically confirmed hypersensitivity to **0% vs 2.7%**, p<0.001, in a predominantly White cohort. Test result is not the whole clinical assessment; prospective screening evidence here is unusually compelling. [S24]
- **CYP2C9/VKORC1 (plus CYP4F2)–warfarin:** Strong dosing/exposure biology, but clinical benefit of testing depends on clinical algorithm, reference group, available variants and INR monitoring. Standard variants undersample CYP2C9 alleles prevalent in African-ancestry populations. Genetics **does not replace INR**. [S2,S25]
- **CYP3A5–tacrolimus:** *1 expressor vs *3/*3 nonexpressor often differs in dose-adjusted trough concentrations; guidance only when genotype is available, alongside mandatory therapeutic drug monitoring. [S26]
- **MT-RNR1 m.1555A>G–aminoglycosides:** Mitochondrial variant associated with medication-related hearing loss; recent FDA table update includes additional aminoglycosides. Mitochondrial heteroplasmy and assay scope add complexity. [S2,S27]
- **NAT2–hydralazine:** CPIC 2025 recognizes acetylator phenotype effects for treatment of resistant hypertension, a relevant but narrow cardiometabolic example. [S17]

## 3. Outcome evidence versus promising biomarkers

**Strong clinical-utility illustration:** PREDICT-1's reduction in immunologically confirmed abacavir hypersensitivity is an intervention outcome, not just a pharmacokinetic biomarker. **Broader but qualified utility:** The PREPARE 12-gene European implementation study (n=6,944 enrolled; open-label cluster-randomized crossover) reported clinically relevant adverse drug reactions in carriers of an actionable interaction in **21.0%** of genotype-guided recipients versus **27.7%** of controls, OR **0.70 (95% CI 0.54–0.91)**; among all analyzed recipients, **21.5% vs 28.6%, OR 0.70 (95% CI 0.61–0.79)** at 12 weeks. **97.7%** self-reported European, Mediterranean or Middle Eastern ethnicity. Outcome definitions, implementation/open-label design and limited ancestry diversity constrain generalization to every gene–drug pair or population. Correspondence published in *The Lancet* disputed the degree of demonstrated benefit, so independent replication and scrutiny remain appropriate. [S28]

**Counterexample:** The TAILOR-PCI primary endpoint (above) was directionally favorable but did not meet the prespecified p<0.05 criterion. **Another counterexample:** The 2023 CPIC antidepressant review explicitly found insufficient evidence for SLC6A4/HTR2A-guided prescribing. **Actionability can be medicine-specific** even within the same gene.

## 4. Obesity, cardiometabolic drugs and GLP-1/GIP precision medicine

- **Semaglutide and tirzepatide:** As of this review, **no verified CPIC genotype-specific prescribing guideline or FDA pharmacogenetic association supporting a GLP1R/GIPR prescribing rule** was located. Absence from those resources is not proof genetics has no effect.
- **2026 original GWAS:** In 27,885 23andMe research participants with *self-reported* GLP-1 drug experience, GLP1R rs10305420 (p.Pro7Leu) was associated with ~**0.76 kg additional weight loss per effect allele** (95% CI approximately **0.34–1.27 kg more loss**), p=2.9×10⁻¹⁰. The allele–BMI association replicated in an independent All of Us EHR cohort (n=4,855; p=0.001; effect about −0.47% BMI change), but not in underpowered UK Biobank analysis. A GIPR signal for nausea/vomiting was observed specifically with tirzepatide. The original discovery sample was **78.3% European, 12.9% Latino, 4.2% African American** ancestry, and mostly women (**82.4%**). These findings are biologically interesting but do **not** establish a clinical cutoff, the net benefit of choosing one drug over another, or a validated genetically guided intervention. [S29]
- **2023 systematic review:** Fourteen studies qualitatively, seven meta-analyzed; variant associations were directionally inconsistent and no consistent overall single-nucleotide variant effect emerged for weight loss. A 2026 review similarly found heterogeneous/inconsistent results among candidate genes. [S30,S31]
- **Metformin:** Transporter and metabolic-gene associations have been researched (e.g., SLC22A1/OCT1, ATM), but no specific ancestry-derived metformin response or genotype dosing should be offered without guideline-level drug-specific support and proven utility.
- **Other anti-obesity medications:** No evidence justifies an ancestry-based prescription rule for orlistat or combination anti-obesity treatments. CYP2D6 pharmacology of an ingredient cannot be extrapolated into an obesity-drug weight-loss predictor.

**Research tier:** GLP1R rs10305420 → `observational` (replicated *association*, not routine care); GIPR rs1800437 and nausea with tirzepatide → `exploratory` (agent-specific, not clinically actionable); old candidate genes → `contradicted` **only for the broad claim that they reproducibly predict individual weight-loss response**, otherwise `exploratory/insufficient`. A validated genotype-based treatment-selection rule is **currently insufficient**.

### Minimum validation before GLP-1 genetics is actionable

Preregister prospective cohorts with accurately verified agent, dose, persistence and indications; use observed rather than only self-reported weight; model baseline BMI, diabetes status and concomitant drugs; replicate ancestry-stratified effect and calibration with adequate counts; test comparative effectiveness of genotype-based *drug choice* versus standard care, absolute benefit and harm; assess economic utility, discrimination and consent. Even a statistically genome-wide-significant association of <1 kg per allele does not demonstrate meaningful individual treatment selection.

## 5. Population and genetic-testing caveats

1. **Ancestry ≠ genotype.** Race and self-described heritage are heterogeneous social, genealogical and geographic constructs. An admixture percentage cannot be converted into a tested allele or a probability precise enough for prescribing.
2. **Allele frequency vs carrier frequency vs phenotype frequency** are distinct denominators. HLA carrier rates, single-allele frequencies and metabolic phenotypes must not be compared without labels. Avoid inferring carriers from allele frequencies using Hardy–Weinberg assumptions in recently admixed or small structured samples.
3. **Population specificity depends on assay coverage.** CYP2D6 structural variants, rare CYP2C9 alleles, DPYD variants and differentiated HLA alleles may be missed by a panel selected for majority-European samples. A negative limited panel is not a proof of normal function.
4. **Phasing and reference build matter.** Star alleles reflect multi-variant haplotypes. Store PharmVar ID, star allele/suballele, rs/HGVS, GRCh37/38, strand, genotype method and date/version. NUDT15 nomenclature has already changed in 2026.
5. **Phenoconversion matters.** CYP inhibitors can make an otherwise normal-genotype individual behave pharmacokinetically like a poor metabolizer; renal function, liver function and concurrent disease matter.
6. **Sampling matters.** Several categories are underrepresented, particularly many African, South Asian, Indigenous American and admixed communities. Aggregated labels hide within-group geographic structure and differences between U.S. and ancestral-country sampling.
7. **Screening advice is not a population-wide alert.** ACR/label *testing opportunity* language is appropriate for an educational page; direct medication-safety judgments based on a user's ancestry are not.

See `03_Population_Medication_Response.md` for source-qualified frequency examples and their distinct sampling definitions.

## 6. What this research does **not** establish

- That a single ancestry-related “metabolizer type” predicts responses across multiple medications.
- That *all* prescriptions for a gene should be managed the same way.
- That clinically recognized PGx relationships warrant universal genetic testing.
- That standard ancestry DNA uploads are clinically interpretable for CYP2D6 CNVs, HLA alleles, DPYD haplotypes or pharmacogenetic treatment decisions.
- That genetic effects are larger than adherence, drug interactions or illness-related factors.
- That GLP1R/GIPR variants can currently choose semaglutide versus tirzepatide or change a recommended weight target.
- That these research documents or the existing calculator are validated clinical decision-support devices.

## 7. Evidence gaps worth researching next

- **Assay sensitivity equity:** Compare expanded versus limited CYP2D6/DPYD/CYP2C9/SLCO1B1 panels across prospectively recruited diverse cohorts.
- **Risk calibration:** Report absolute adverse-reaction rates by confirmed genotype and clinical covariates, not only large ORs for rare HLA hypersensitivity events.
- **GLP-1 replication:** Establish genotype-by-agent interaction, objective efficacy, validated side-effect ascertainment and decision utility; estimate calibration separately for East Asian, South Asian, African, European and admixed American cohorts.
- **Multimorbidity:** Study whether PGx benefit changes when obesity, diabetes, CKD, psychiatric illness, cardiovascular disease and polypharmacy coexist; avoid coupling their algorithms without causal evidence.
- **Implementation science:** Compare clinician comprehension, unnecessary alerts, unsafe self-adjustment intent, resource burden and disparities with/without educational content.

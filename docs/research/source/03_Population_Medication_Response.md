# Research Team 3 — Population Differences in Medication Response

**Evidence cut-off:** 1 November 2026  
**Scope:** allele-frequency variation, pharmacogenetic interpretation and equity. **Not** individualized clinical assessment, diagnosis, prescribing or ancestry-based risk scoring.  
**Companion matrix:** `03_Pharmacogenetics_Evidence_Matrix.md` (PGX-001–PGX-036).

## Executive interpretation

Population genetics matters because allele frequencies and assay detection rates differ, sometimes sharply. It does **not** follow that everyone in a population has a corresponding allele, that drug responses are determined by ethnicity, or that genotype can be predicted from the existing calculator's self-reported ancestry percentages. Even when a medically meaningful allele is common within a sampled population, many people do not carry it; conversely, a rare allele can occur in any group. Genetic admixture, regional origins, selection of comparison populations and clinical context complicate all broad labels.

The safest design is to use population research to **explain why screening policies and panel comprehensiveness differ**, to make underrepresentation visible, and to encourage clinicians to review current guidelines when genuinely relevant. It should not generate individual predictions.

## 1. Five concepts that must not be conflated

1. **Allele frequency** is the fraction of assayed chromosome copies carrying an allele. For autosomal markers, this differs from **carrier prevalence**: the fraction of people with at least one copy. A 10% allele frequency is not automatically a 10% carrier prevalence. Carrier conversion also requires assumptions about mating patterns, data quality and genotype counts.
2. **Genotype/diplotype** describes an individual's observed variants and two-allele combination (or copy number); functional phenotypes such as CYP2D6 poor metabolizer depend on the correctly resolved genotype **and functional definitions**.
3. **Self-reported race/ethnicity**, **self-reported ancestral origin** and **genetic ancestry estimates** are different measurements. None is a laboratory call for HLA-B*15:02, CYP2C19*2 or another variant.
4. **Clinical outcome frequency** among medication users is not directly the allele frequency. A severe reaction depends on exposure, indication, age, comorbidity, co-medications, test methods, and other factors.
5. **Screening policy** (whether testing is recommended in a particular context) is distinct from **genotype-directed therapy guidance** (what a clinician might do with an existing validated test result).

## 2. Strongest population-differentiated examples

### HLA-B*15:02 — carbamazepine-associated SJS/TEN (PGX-003, PGX-023)

The U.S. carbamazepine label reports **approximate percentages of people testing positive** for HLA-B*15:02: greater than 15% in selected samples from Hong Kong, Thailand, Malaysia and parts of the Philippines; approximately 10% in Taiwan; 4% in North China; approximately 2–4% for South Asian samples including India, with higher prevalence in some communities; and less than 1% in Japanese and Korean populations. These are *carrier/positive percentages described by the label*, not a universal East Asian allele frequency or a drug-reaction incidence. Importantly, there is substantial heterogeneity between neighboring regions within the same broad ancestry label.

**Clinical boundary:** HLA-B*15:02 strongly associates with carbamazepine-triggered SJS/TEN, and CPIC and the drug label provide clinically significant guidance. The label discusses testing people with ancestry in populations where this allele occurs, but ancestry is an imperfect screening context rather than an individual molecular result. A negative HLA-B*15:02 result does not rule out other hypersensitivity mechanisms, including HLA-A*31:01-associated reactions.

Source: [2026 DailyMed carbamazepine labeling](https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=50ba2042-fc67-4266-a773-a30f07649a5a).

### HLA-B*58:01 — allopurinol severe cutaneous reactions (PGX-005, PGX-006, PGX-024, PGX-034)

Allopurinol labeling reports **approximate population frequencies** of HLA-B*58:01: 8–10% among Han Chinese samples, about 8% Thai, 6% Korean, 4% Black populations, 1–2% Indigenous American/Hispanic and under 1% European/Japanese. The label's generic term “frequency” needs primary-source harmonization before conversion to a comparative allele- or carrier-frequency graph. The 2020 American College of Rheumatology guideline instead reports prevalence of 7.4% in combined Han Chinese/Korean/Thai samples, 3.8% among African Americans, and 0.7% for White and Hispanic groups—numbers drawn from different underlying data, not directly interchangeable with the product label.

ACR **conditionally recommends** HLA-B*58:01 testing before starting allopurinol for patients of Southeast Asian descent (including examples labeled Han Chinese, Korean or Thai) and African American patients; it **conditionally recommends against universal testing in others**. The certainty for its screening-policy recommendation is described as *very low*. That is separate from the strong molecular association and CPIC's recommendations when HLA status is known. A very large meta-analyzed odds ratio for severe reactions does **not** equate to the absolute probability that a carrier will react.

Sources: [2026 DailyMed allopurinol labeling](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cdc3f33b-ff2b-4a84-a820-51a497fdf3d9); [2020 ACR guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10563586/); [2025 association meta-analysis (PMID 40841814)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40841814/).

### CYP2C19 — clopidogrel and selected SSRIs (PGX-001, PGX-012, PGX-025)

The following are **illustrative allele-copy frequencies** summarized in a cross-population source; estimates are source/population-specific, approximate, and may be affected by cohort selection. They must **not** be reinterpreted as the chance that a person is an intermediate or poor metabolizer.

| CYP2C19 allele | East Asian comparison samples | European comparison samples | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `*2` (no function) | ~0.28 | ~0.15 | Often more common in East Asian samples |
| `*3` (no function) | ~0.07 | ~0.002 | Especially variable by region and subgroup |
| `*17` (increased function) | ~0.02 | ~0.22 | Often relatively more common in European samples |

Source: [Cross-population review, allele frequency table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12850553/). South and Central Asian samples are not interchangeable with East Asian samples: the cited synthesis reports potentially high *2 frequencies, but distributions and representative populations must be reviewed before numerical release. African populations show different *2/*17 distributions, and admixed American subpopulations vary with ancestral contributions and cohort selection. **Do not assign any star allele to an individual based on their origin**. Clinical action depends on confirmed *diplotype*, whether the individual is treated with clopidogrel or a particular antidepressant, and the clinical indication.

### CYP2D6 — codeine, tramadol, atomoxetine, some antidepressants and metoprolol (PGX-009–PGX-012, PGX-031)

CYP2D6 variation is more complicated than a few SNP frequencies. Copy-number variation (including whole-gene deletions/duplications and hybrid arrangements), no-function and decreased-function alleles, and inhibitor-induced **phenoconversion** determine functional drug metabolism. The decreased-function `*10` pattern is frequently reported in East Asian cohorts; `*17`/`*29` are important in many African ancestry cohorts; `*4` is a common European no-function allele. These are **tendencies in source-defined datasets**, not biological traits that define entire populations. Some South Asian and admixed American datasets have sparse or poorly resolved structural-variant coverage.

A panel that tests only a European-enriched variant subset can return misleading “normal” phenotype calls elsewhere. Ethnic origin does not resolve copy number; genetic ancestry proportions do not provide the sequence of both copies; and CYP2D6 inhibitors can change effective activity despite a normal genotype. See [PharmVar](https://www.pharmvar.org/) and the [CPIC opioid guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8249478/).

### TPMT and NUDT15 — thiopurine toxicity (PGX-007, PGX-008)

TPMT decreased-function variants and NUDT15 variants both raise susceptibility to thiopurine-related bone marrow toxicity. They have **different** allele frequency distributions, with NUDT15 decreased-function variants particularly relevant in several East Asian and some Hispanic/Indigenous American populations and TPMT variants detected in many European and other cohorts. Importantly, *both genes matter across ancestries*. Testing the gene assumed to be most common in a patient's perceived ethnicity alone may miss a medically important variant. The CPIC **2025 updated guideline, published 2026**, also adjusted NUDT15 nomenclature and combined-phenotype interpretation. [Guideline DOI:10.1002/cpt.70209](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.70209).

### DPYD — fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy (PGX-016–PGX-018)

Many existing panels emphasize variants originally studied in European-enriched cohorts. That is useful but incomplete: reduced-function variants including `c.557A>G` have relevance to people with African ancestral contributions. An apparent “negative” result from a narrow panel is not proof of normal DPD activity or absence of severe-toxicity risk. The July 2026 CPIC *interim* reevaluation of the HapB3 variant is a reminder that genotype-to-function assignment can change with evidence. Panel inclusivity and test analytic sensitivity require review across **specific sampled populations**, not a binary racial flag. Sources: [DPYD diversity and panel review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11300675/); [CPIC interim update](https://www.clinpgx.org/guidelineAnnotation/PA166122686).

### SLCO1B1, ABCG2 and CYP2C9 — statins (PGX-014, PGX-015)

Transporter and enzyme allele frequencies differ across world populations, but the relevant clinical mechanism is **particular genotype + particular statin**, with indication, exposure and interactions. For example `ABCG2` rs2231142 can modify rosuvastatin exposure, while `SLCO1B1` rs4149056 influences some statin exposures and muscle toxicity. A frequency atlas must not flatten this into “Asians have higher statin risks” or “Europeans tolerate statins.” CPIC 2022 covers SLCO1B1, ABCG2 and CYP2C9 in a drug-specific manner: [DOI:10.1002/cpt.2557](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.2557).

### CYP3A5 and warfarin — an important equity check (PGX-020, PGX-021)

CYP3A5 `*1` expressor alleles can be common in some African ancestry cohorts; `*3` nonexpression is more frequent in many European samples, while `*6` and `*7` must not be overlooked when analyzing African ancestry cohorts. Tacrolimus therapeutic drug monitoring remains indispensable irrespective of genotype. For warfarin, VKORC1/CYP2C9 genetic effects are established, but algorithms developed around `CYP2C9 *2/*3` can inadequately represent `*5/*6/*8/*11` in other populations, potentially worsening equity. Genotype-informed algorithms and clinical INR monitoring are **not substitutes for each other**.

Sources: [CPIC tacrolimus PMID 25801146](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25801146/); [CPIC guidelines list](https://cpicpgx.org/guidelines/).

## 3. Outcome disparities are not necessarily genetic effects

Among allopurinol users in one large population-based study, severe cutaneous reactions differed by recorded racial/ethnic categories. Such observations can motivate investigation, but **cannot isolate** HLA allele frequency from kidney disease, dose/exposure, health-system factors, access to testing, co-medications, ascertainment and other confounders. A statistically significant ethnic association may therefore have **no valid translation into an individual's genotype or drug risk**. Source: [Allopurinol-related severe cutaneous reactions in U.S. Medicaid study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6045437/).

The 2026 GLP1R study also reported different unadjusted weight-loss patterns by ancestry group. Its sample was predominantly female and approximately 78% European ancestry; smaller non-European strata limit inference, and the modest `GLP1R` rs10305420 association is **not an ancestry-based treatment-selection rule**. Any between-group differences can reflect baseline diabetes, treatment type, dose, follow-up, selection, socioeconomic factors, weight-recording differences and structural disparities. [Nature 2026 DOI:10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z).

## 4. Admixed American populations require particular care

“Latino,” “Hispanic,” “Central American” and “South American” cover multiple distinct social identities and varied ancestral histories; African, Indigenous American, European and other components differ widely between and within countries and families. Two individuals with identical percentage ancestry summaries can have opposite genotypes at a clinically relevant locus. The source of genotyping may be a consumer SNP array, clinical sequencing panel or specialist HLA assay, each with different detection capability. Do not calculate an **expected carrier genotype** from ancestry mix. Do not treat nationality, language or appearance as a proxy for SNP, HLA type or CYP2D6 structural variants.

Likewise, “African ancestry” is not homogeneous: genetic diversity across African regions is high, and the available commercial variant panels often underrepresent many locally common or rare haplotypes. “South Asian” may combine people from genetically and culturally different regions. “European” is also too coarse for rare variants; subdividing Scandinavian/Mediterranean/Central European without robust locus-specific cohorts adds apparent precision without increased validity.

## 5. Acceptable vs unacceptable population-facing features

| Acceptable educational experience | Unacceptable clinical inference |
|---|---|
| “HLA-B*15:02 frequencies vary greatly among sampled East Asian regions; here are the source and caveats.” | “You selected East Asian, so you carry the allele.” |
| “ACR 2020 conditionally recommends selected-group allopurinol screening; consult a qualified clinician.” | “Your ancestry makes allopurinol unsafe, so stop or change it.” |
| “This study reports the frequency of CYP2C19*2 *allele copies* in a defined cohort.” | “Your probability of clopidogrel failure is 28%.” |
| “A test panel may fail to detect structural CYP2D6 variants.” | “No listed variants means normal metabolism.” |
| “A GLP1R variant correlates with modest weight-loss differences in research cohorts.” | “Your ancestry predicts which GLP-1/GIP medicine to take or what dose you need.” |

## 6. Minimum data quality requirements for future population tables

Each population-frequency datum should preserve: `frequency_id`, `PGX_claim_id`, allele nomenclature/reference assembly, allele vs carrier vs genotype prevalence, numerator/denominator, geographic/source population, ancestry *measurement method*, sample selection, age/sex where relevant, genotyping technology, study year, CI or uncertainty (or explicit `not reported`), source DOI, source table/figure, date accessed, transferability caveats and last medical review. Prefer peer-reviewed primary sampling datasets with directly reported counts; label-reported aggregate percentages should be clearly labeled as **label summaries**, and should not be graphed as if they came from one harmonized study.

**Validation checks:** (1) do not silently mix percentage types; (2) avoid ancestry-fraction-to-genotype formulas; (3) visually flag underpowered cohort sizes; (4) no population group is hidden when its frequency is unreported—state *not established*; (5) do not impute any individual drug response; (6) require independent pharmacist/PGx geneticist audit.

## Research priorities

1. Reconstruct primary HLA sample denominators and uncertainty for subgroups rather than republish only broad regional percentages.
2. Conduct a diverse-assay coverage audit for CYP2D6 structural variants, DPYD, TPMT and NUDT15, including admixed American, sub-Saharan African, South Asian and Indigenous American datasets.
3. Systematically evaluate whether any population-specific **testing policy** improves equitable outcomes; distinguish screening access from proxy prescribing.
4. Track the 2026 GLP1R/GIPR associations with multi-ancestry prospective replication and well-described treatment exposures; do not propose ancestry-specific semaglutide or tirzepatide dosing.

# Rights, safety, cultural stewardship and hypothesis hygiene

This track is historical and ethnographic research, **not medical advice**.

## Ethical review before collection

- The GitHub repository is public. Record only a source identifier, URL and original *short paraphrase* pending permission; do not mirror full copyrighted texts, audio or pictures.
- Respect [CARE Principles](https://www.gida-global.org/careprinciples) for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics), including collective rights and cultural authority beyond Western copyright. Some apparently public narratives remain sensitive or subject to community-imposed restrictions.
- A repository license cannot supersede archival license or cultural access conditions. The Dúchas Schools' Collection displays institution-specific rights and open-data conditions; verify per material before copying, transformation or machine processing.
- Do not include names of living narrators, interviewees, or clinical subjects without legitimate basis/consent. Historical names require proportionality, not blind copying.
- Maintain a contact / takedown workflow before large ingestion. If a source objects, restrict access and quarantine derivatives.
- Preserve original language and date context; machine translations should be labelled and reviewed by language/cultural experts.

## Risk and medical boundaries

- **Never** infer diseases, ancestry/genetic traits, disease prevalence, or treatment effectiveness from a legend, ethnic category, or cosmology.
- Retellings may describe trauma, disabilities, psychiatric symptoms, pregnancy outcomes, sudden deaths or violence. Avoid sensationalism and stigmatization, especially when compiling "cursed" accounts.
- Many folk cures can be poisonous, infectious, or harmful. Archive descriptions only; **do not recommend or operationalize traditional treatments**.
- Use a strong counterfactual: if two narratives resemble the same disease, do shared literary motifs, cultural contact, or source-copying better explain the similarity?
- Avoid retrospective diagnosis of individuals without clinical evidence, and do not draw causal conclusions from anecdote.
- Differentiate narrative categories: direct witness account, hearsay, literary fable, explanatory tradition, ritual protocol, and modern retelling. Record uncertain status explicitly.

## Release gates

1. Candidate lead and specific attestation source identified.
2. Provenance and relationships checked, including direct copying and translations.
3. Rights and cultural governance cleared.
4. Medical observations extracted separately from the narrative explanation.
5. Multiple plausible explanations and negative evidence identified.
6. Anthropological/community experts and clinically appropriate reviewers assess.
7. Only independently supported evidence is nominated for the **separate** Atlas clinical evidence process. Story intake cannot write clinical decisions or calculators.

## Concrete comparative case

A sleep-pressure tradition may involve waking immobility, chest tightness, or perceived presence: compare sleep-paralysis studies with cultural accounts. Sudden nocturnal death requires **different** evidence and cannot be equated with a nightmare. See [sleep-paralysis cross-cultural review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5013036/) and [Philippine bangungot paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3404490/).

## Rights field rules

- `unknown`: no reuse assumption.
- `link_only`: discovery metadata and a link only; no mirroring.
- `licensed_for_specific_use`: require license URI, terms, attribution, scope, revocation/embargo.
- `permission_required`: nothing stored beyond safe locator while rights review occurs.
- `restricted` or `do_not_archive`: do not retain sensitive content.
